    Chapter 7 Long division again

    The division sheet has been waiting for us. Its rules still say to bring down a zero, subtract the divisor as many times as possible, and write the number of successful subtractions. Nothing in the intervening chapters has taught a remainder to behave differently. What has become less straightforward is getting the two integers onto the page.
    🔗
    For the moment, we can supply them ourselves. Suppose we want the decimal to begin with 314. The drawer runs from \(0.314\) to \(0.315\text{.}\) Halfway across sits
    \begin{equation*} 0.3145=\frac{629}{2000}. \end{equation*}
    Here is the old procedure at work on those integers:
    🔗
    place ten times old remainder digit new remainder
    1 6290 3 290
    2 2900 1 900
    3 9000 4 1000
    4 10000 5 0
    The first line subtracts three copies of 2000 from 6290. The 290 left over becomes 2900 on the next line, where one copy fits. Then four copies fit, then five. We have our 314, followed by a five and a remainder of zero. More steps would write zeros.
    🔗
    We could have asked for 003. Taking the midpoint again gives \(0.0035=7/2000\text{.}\) A numerator of seven has no zeros at its front, but dividing by 2000 makes the worker write them. Three places were requested, and the denominator remembers how many. For a word of \(k\) digits whose integer value is \(a\text{,}\) the same recipe makes
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2a+1}{2\cdot10^k} =\frac{a}{10^k}+\frac{1}{2\cdot10^k}. \end{equation*}
    The term on the left of the plus sign is the drawer’s lower end; the other term moves halfway across. The worker doubles \(a\text{,}\) adds one, and divides by a two followed by \(k\) zeros. The answer begins with the requested word, then a five, then zeros.
    🔗
    Even 000 and 999 cause no difficulty:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{2000}=0.0005, \qquad \frac{1999}{2000}=0.9995. \end{equation*}
    A finite beginning is easily accommodated. The difficulty we have been pursuing belongs to the whole endless writing. We defeated every ordinary fraction in the decimal game, then every ordinary digit-printing program. Yet here is division, willing to reproduce whatever finite evidence of our victory we put in front of it.
    🔗
    So far the requested word has chosen the numerator and denominator. We will reverse the arrangement. Two instruction sheets, \(P\) and \(Q\text{,}\) will be fixed before any infinite decimal is chosen. Their job is to supply the two integers. They get their material from an unlikely source: a proof that their division cannot begin with some finite word. Give them an objection to 314 and they will prepare 629 and 2000. The objection has included the very digits needed to answer it.
    🔗
    We have met this response before in \(E\text{.}\) What is new is the need to keep a whole calculation together. A claim that one program has an output, another has an output, and some fraction somewhere begins with 314 would leave too much room between the pieces. The division must use those outputs, and a later request must refer to that same division.
    🔗
    Take the completed work for 629 and 2000. Alongside the two printer records, save how their written numerals acquire their values. Reading 629 from left to right gives
    \begin{equation*} 0\longrightarrow6\longrightarrow62\longrightarrow629. \end{equation*}
    At each arrow the value so far is multiplied by ten and the next digit added. The denominator’s chain is \(0,2,20,200,2000\text{.}\) Let \(p\) and \(q\) denote the last values. The record requires \(q>0\) and \(0\leq p<q\text{,}\) and attaches a division of precisely these integers, starting with \(0.\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The division table retains the connection to its numerator at every line. For our example the checks are
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 10\cdot629&=3\cdot2000+290,\\ 100\cdot629&=31\cdot2000+900,\\ 1000\cdot629&=314\cdot2000+1000,\\ 10000\cdot629&=3145\cdot2000+0. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    The remainders remain nonnegative and smaller than 2000. This is Chapter 2’s invariant, carrying the original division through its changing answer row. The third line puts \(1000\cdot629\) between \(314\cdot2000\) and \(315\cdot2000\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} 0.314<\frac{p}{q}<0.315. \end{equation*}
    The first two lines similarly place the very same quotient in the drawers for 3 and 31. We have not changed fractions as the answer became more specific.
    🔗
    Call the joined records a packet, and give the packet the name \(d\text{.}\) Its checks include the two completed runs, the values of their outputs, and a division record ending at a digit boundary with remainder zero. It may keep further zero-writing steps after the first zero remainder. For a finite word \(s\text{,}\) abbreviate the claim we want by
    \begin{equation*} \Phi_s=\exists d\, \bigl(\operatorname{Record}(d)\land\operatorname{Prefix}_s(d)\bigr). \end{equation*}
    It says that one such packet exists and its division starts with \(s\text{.}\) The prefix part checks the digits and the interval inequalities for that same \(p,q\text{.}\) It also requires enough recorded places. Asking for a thousand zeros demands a record of a thousand places, even though the answer row would be easy to describe in fewer words.
    🔗
    We arrange these requirements so that a longer word keeps the earlier checks. The request for 3141 includes the request for 314, then adds a fourth digit and the narrower interval. Our packet for \(629/2000\) fails the addition: its fourth digit is five. Any packet that passes, however, still passes every earlier check. The formulas join the old conditions to the new ones with “and,” so our proof rule can take the old conditions back out. This small feature will keep one calculation in view when the requests become endless.
    🔗
    Our proof method \(T\) and its fixed record coding must be able to handle this packet. We retain the assumptions from Chapter 5: \(T\) is consistent, its certificates can be mechanically enumerated, and actual finite successful work can be translated into proofs of the corresponding record claims. Here that work includes the combined calculation, not just two isolated outputs. Across models the coding must also retain the stated ordered arithmetic and the deterministic changes of configuration. These are requirements on the interpretation used in our adjunctive setting, set out in Chapter 2 of the Technical Companion; consistency by itself does not give an arbitrary theory this ability to talk about computation. We will use the requirements at the two places where they are needed: to verify a hypothetical ordinary answer, and to understand a model’s extraordinary one.
    🔗
    The search can now have a definite target. It lists finite words beside candidate certificates, checks the certificates, and looks for a conclusion of the form \(\neg\Phi_s\text{.}\) Such a conclusion forbids a common packet with the beginning \(s\text{.}\) When the search finds one, it returns the word. The numerator worker doubles its value and adds one; the denominator worker writes a two followed by as many zeros as the word has places. Both follow the same ordered search from empty input.
    🔗
    One knot remains. The sentences being searched for must concern these very workers. Kleene’s copying construction lets us tie it without asking either worker to recognize itself in a mirror. Recall the white and blue sheets: a supplied text was copied into a package, and the other sheet supplied the text that made the package reconstruct its own description. The task could then use that description as ordinary input.
    🔗
    For our present task, take a proposed controller text \(e\text{.}\) Write a description \(P_e\) saying to obey \(e\text{,}\) take its returned word, and perform the numerator job. Write \(Q_e\) with the denominator job instead. Preparing these descriptions requires copying instructions, not waiting for them to finish. Using the two descriptions, the task writes the formulas for their common records and starts the search for exclusions. Kleene supplies a controller \(e\) carrying out this task on its own description. The workers it describes therefore begin by obeying the controller that has just described them.
    🔗
    Follow the response once, assuming a certificate arrives. The worker on \(P_e\) runs the controller; the controller reconstructs the printer descriptions and searches for an exclusion. Suppose the first excludes 003. It returns that word, and \(P_e\) calculates \(2\cdot3+1=7\text{.}\) The identical search inside \(Q_e\) returns 003 too, so \(Q_e\) counts its three places and writes 2000. The quotient is \(0.0035\text{,}\) as required. The copying construction has supplied descriptions of this behavior without presuming that a certificate ever arrives.
    🔗
    Fix the resulting sheets and call them \(P,Q\text{.}\) They depend on the proof method \(T\text{,}\) whose checker they use. They do not depend on any chosen infinite decimal.
    🔗
    Now suppose an exclusion certificate exists. Take the first the search would find; say it excludes 314. Reaching that particular certificate takes an ordinary finite amount of work. The check, the return of the word, the printing of 629 and 2000, and the four division lines also take ordinary finite work. Together they give exactly the packet above. Our verification assumption turns it into a \(T\)-proof of \(\Phi_{314}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The certificate was a \(T\)-proof of \(\neg\Phi_{314}\text{.}\) Consistency forbids the pair. Nothing depended on those three digits: whichever finite word appeared first, the response would build its midpoint fraction and verify a packet with that beginning. There is therefore no ordinary exclusion certificate for any finite word. Our printers remain in their searches forever. We have designed two integer printers whose answer rows stay blank, and the proof of their usefulness has begun by establishing this defect.
    🔗
    For each particular \(s\text{,}\) the absence of an exclusion proof also tells us that \(T+\Phi_s\) is consistent. If this extra assumption led to a contradiction, closing the assumption would give a proof of \(\neg\Phi_s\) in \(T\text{,}\) the certificate we just ruled out. Henkin consequently supplies a model of \(T+\Phi_s\text{,}\) with a packet realizing that finite beginning. We have models for 314, for 3141, and for any finite continuation we care to name.
    🔗
    Separate models still leave us short of the promised decimal. One model could supply three places, another four; each new digit would send us shopping for another calculation. Instead choose an entire infinite digit sequence, perhaps beginning \(31415926\ldots\text{,}\) and give the packet a single new name, \(d\text{.}\) The new notebook contains \(T\text{,}\) the assertion that \(d\) is a checked packet, and these requests:
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Prefix}_{3}(d),\quad \operatorname{Prefix}_{31}(d),\quad \operatorname{Prefix}_{314}(d),\quad \operatorname{Prefix}_{3141}(d),\quad\ldots. \end{equation*}
    Each entry uses the same name. The list runs through every ordinary finite beginning of the chosen sequence.
    🔗
    Take any finite selection of these entries. It has a longest requested beginning. We already have a model of \(T\) with a packet for that word; interpret \(d\) as that packet. Because its prefix checks include all the shorter ones, this one packet meets every request in the selection. It also meets the common-record requirements. Thus every finite selection can be satisfied together.
    🔗
    Compactness gives one model satisfying the entire notebook. Its packet contains one pair of completed printer runs, one pair of integer values \(p,q\text{,}\) and their division. Every ordinary finite request applies to that same packet. This is where the fastening of the records pays for itself: the fractions cannot change between one digit and the next.
    🔗
    The order of our choices is part of the result:
    🔗
    Fix a consistent, mechanically enumerable classical first-order theory \(T\) and coding with the arithmetic, deterministic-record, and finite-verification properties described above. There are two fixed programs \(P,Q\) such that, for every external infinite decimal sequence, some model of \(T\) contains one completed record of their integer outputs and their division, agreeing with that sequence at every ordinary finite position.
    🔗
    🔗
    The programs are fixed before the sequence. The model may depend on the sequence, and need not extend a particular model we already have. An uncomputable sequence supplies no algorithm for writing all the requests into Henkin’s notebook; the sequence need not even be one of the eventual model’s objects. We used its finite beginnings to prove the existence of a suitable setting.
    🔗
    The quotient is rational in that setting. Our first escape in the decimal game was from rational numbers. It is worth staying with this apparent collision until we can locate it on the division sheet.
    🔗
    The packet has a last saved line, with remainder zero. Suppose it were line one thousand. Our notebook also asks that the same packet contain the first thousand and one digits. Suppose it were line one million; a longer request would reach past that too. The last line’s index, \(b\text{,}\) must exceed every ordinary numeral. Inside the model this is a finite record that reaches zero and would thereafter print zeros. None of its ordinary positions has to be where that final tail begins.
    🔗
    Choose the familiar growing-gap decimal:
    \begin{equation*} 0.01001000100001000001\ldots. \end{equation*}
    At every ordinary position the model’s division agrees with it. There is always a later ordinary one, and another longer gap. The zero tail lies beyond all those positions. There is no first extra position immediately after ordinary counting, just as there was no first nonstandard integer in the last chapter. If \(b-1\) were an ordinary position, then so would \(b\) be; the same reasoning holds for \(b-2\text{,}\) \(b-3\text{,}\) and each ordinary distance we count backward. The record’s internal end does not furnish an ordinary place at which our target begins to repeat.
    🔗
    The printer records have the same separation from ordinary time. Their starting configurations are fixed, and their decoded successors are prescribed by the instructions. At any ordinary stage, repeating those checks identifies exactly the search we could perform at the desk. Neither search halts there. A model’s completed record includes an internally accepted exclusion certificate; if that were an ordinary finite certificate we could check, we would recover the contradiction above. No new ordinary answer has appeared on either worker’s paper.
    🔗
    The nines give the difference a numerical shape. Ask successively for 9, for 99, for 999. Our midpoint fractions are
    \begin{equation*} \frac{19}{20}=0.95,\qquad \frac{199}{200}=0.995,\qquad \frac{1999}{2000}=0.9995. \end{equation*}
    Each sits below one, with gaps \(1/20\text{,}\) \(1/200\text{,}\) and \(1/2000\text{.}\) Put all the nine-requests into the notebook and use compactness as before. Its common quotient still satisfies \(p/q<1\text{,}\) because the record requires \(p<q\text{,}\) but every ordinary position is a nine.
    🔗
    Let \(h=1-p/q\) be its positive gap. The request for \(n+1\) nines puts the quotient at least as high as \(1-10^{-(n+1)}\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} 0<h\leq\frac{1}{10^{n+1}}<\frac{1}{10^n} \end{equation*}
    for every ordinary positive integer \(n\text{.}\) A gap smaller than each of these ordinary decimal scales can exist in the model’s ordered arithmetic. It is not a positive ordinary real number. Two distinct ordinary reals have some decimal scale fine enough to separate them.
    🔗
    Thus \(0.999\ldots=1\) remains exactly right when those nines name an ordinary real. The quotient in our model lives in a larger ordered setting; agreement at every ordinary decimal place does not identify an element of that setting. We may likewise request \(0.3000\ldots\) or \(0.2999\ldots\text{,}\) the two ordinary names for the same endpoint. Both writings are allowed targets, possibly realized in different models. The promise concerns the digits at ordinary positions.
    🔗
    Now use the halting decimal from Chapter 4. Its positions answer our numbered questions about programs and inputs: does this run stop? We proved that no ordinary program prints all those answers. The same fixed \(P,Q\) nevertheless have a suitable model with one division record agreeing with each answer at its ordinary position. Its model need not call that record its own halting sequence. The target answers were chosen outside it. At our desk, the searches still do not finish.
    🔗
    The certificate reversal adapts the argument Hamkins attributes to Vadim Kosoy. Keeping the outputs and quotient in a common record lets the finite-prefix claims pass together into compactness. The source notes distinguish this suitable-model result from the stronger extension results known for universal algorithms. Our claim remains conditional on the stated arithmetic and record-verification properties.
    🔗
    We began with a person who could do a calculation without understanding what it meant. That separation made the work checkable: a borrowed one belonged in its column regardless of anyone’s opinion about numbers. Every ordinary step retains that exactness. But the claim that all the steps form a completed finite computation brings the surrounding world into the sentence. The instructions fit on their familiar pages. Whether those pages have a finished answer is no longer a question the pages can settle alone.
