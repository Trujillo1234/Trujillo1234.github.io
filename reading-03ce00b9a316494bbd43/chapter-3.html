    Chapter 3 Drawers and games

    You write two. I add seventeen. You add zero, zero, four. Between us we have made the beginning
    \begin{equation*} 0.2\qquad\longrightarrow\qquad0.217 \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad0.217004. \end{equation*}
    Neither of us had to approve the other’s digits. Now imagine that we want incompatible things from this collaboration. You want the whole decimal to name a fraction. I want it to be irrational. We can each write as many digits as we like on a turn, provided the block is finite and contains at least one digit. Then we have to hand the pencil back. The earlier writing stays.
    🔗
    The number on the table has acquired two authors and no editor. After \(0.217004\text{,}\) zeros forever would make a fraction. A growing-gap tail would make it irrational. Both futures are available, but neither player can simply choose one: the other will interrupt. The question is what either of us can make happen despite those interruptions.
    🔗
    There is no last turn. Because each move adds digits, every particular position eventually fills, and the whole play names a real between zero and one. The all-nines play names one. Our rule gives every completed infinite play a winner, even though the completed page will never lie between our coffee cups.
    🔗
    Coffee has some claim to a place in this picture. In the Scottish Café in Lwów, mathematicians including Stefan Banach and Stanisław Mazur discussed problems recorded in a notebook now known as the Scottish Book. Mazur’s Problem 43, from 1935, offered a bottle of wine for a question connecting a game of shrinking intervals with a mathematical notion of smallness.
     1 
    Problem 43 in Mauldin’s edition of the Scottish Book.
    Appending a block of digits is our way of choosing a smaller range of possibilities. The player gets to decide how much smaller before giving up the pencil.
    🔗
    Take the irrational player’s seat. On the first turn write a zero and a one; on the second write two zeros and a one; on the third, three zeros and a one. Continue lengthening the zero block. The other player can put a thousand sevens between our turns, or attempt to restore a pattern with an elaborate block of their own. They cannot remove our zero runs, whose lengths keep growing, and they cannot make our successive ones cease.
    🔗
    The proof from the previous chapter survives. A repeating block containing a one cannot allow arbitrarily long stretches of zeros. A repeating block containing no one cannot allow ones arbitrarily far along the page. Our moves ensure both, whatever else appears between them. We can force irrationality while leaving much of the actual number to somebody else. The opponent can spoil our intended decimal without spoiling the reason it escapes fractions.
    🔗
    Hold the page at \(0.2\text{.}\) Imagine a drawer labeled \([0.2]\text{,}\) containing every complete decimal paper that could continue this beginning. It holds \(0.20000\ldots\text{,}\) \(0.217004\ldots\text{,}\) \(0.29999\ldots\text{,}\) and every other way of going on after the two. The label tells us what is fixed; inside are all the possibilities still unsettled.
    🔗
    Sort these papers by their next digit, and the drawer divides into ten smaller ones:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{gathered} {}[0.20],\ [0.21],\ [0.22],\ [0.23],\ [0.24],\\ {}[0.25],\ [0.26],\ [0.27],\ [0.28],\ [0.29]. \end{gathered} \end{equation*}
    The drawer \([0.21]\) opens into \([0.210]\) through \([0.219]\text{,}\) each with ten more inside. A finite label never gets us to the back of this cabinet.
    🔗
    Our sample play has selected three nested drawers:
    \begin{equation*} [0.217004]\ \text{inside}\ [0.217]\ \text{inside}\ [0.2]. \end{equation*}
    A move of one digit goes one level deeper. A move of a thousand digits goes a thousand levels deeper. However far the other player goes, their chosen drawer remains inside the one we last chose.
    🔗
    The objects in these drawers are papers, which matters at a shared edge. \([0.20]\) contains \(0.209999\ldots\text{,}\) while \([0.21]\) contains \(0.210000\ldots\text{.}\) The two papers name the same real. Their numerical ranges touch, although the papers themselves have different second digits and sit in different drawers. An event about the number \(0.21\) has to count both papers; an event about the next written digit concerns the particular paper we are making.
    🔗
    By an event, we mean something that can happen in the course of play, or a property of the complete decimal. “A seven appears” is an event. So is “sevens keep appearing, however far along we look.” Our move seventeen settled the first: the seven is visible. It did not settle the second. We may have written the only seven the number will ever have. Events need not reveal themselves all at once, or at any finite point.
    🔗
    Choose an event before play. The first player wants it to happen; the second wants it to fail. It depends on the digits or their value, rather than on which player wrote which mark. Our first event was rationality. We defeated it by spoiling every repeating tail. The drawers suggest a different attack, one that will work against lists much larger than the fractions.
    🔗
    Try to exclude just one half. Suppose the current page begins \(0.49\text{.}\) Writing another nine keeps us away from \(0.5000\ldots\text{,}\) but it leaves the other name, \(0.4999\ldots\text{,}\) perfectly possible. Write zero instead. Now the whole drawer \([0.490]\) misses one half: none of its papers starts with five, and none has the nine required at the third place of \(0.4999\ldots\text{.}\) Every later block stays inside this safe drawer. We have finished with one half, however many digits remain unwritten.
    🔗
    One third is even easier. If the paper says \(0.3\text{,}\) write four. There is now a permanent disagreement with \(0.333\ldots\text{.}\) The other player may answer with a thousand threes, but the four in the second place does not move. A very long reply need not be an effective reply.
    🔗
    We can give every fraction such a turn. Make a list: denominator one with its possible numerators between zero and one; denominator two with numerators zero, one, two; denominator three with zero, one, two, three; and so on. Each stage is finite, and every fraction between zero and one occurs. Repetitions such as \(1/2\) and \(2/4\) do no harm. Beside each entry put all its decimal names beginning with \(0.\text{.}\) Zero contributes its zeros, one its nines, and one half contributes both names. This is still a numbered list.
    🔗
    On our first turn, compare the shared page with the first name on the list. If they already disagree, add any digit—a move is required—and pass the pencil. If they still agree, calculate the next digit of that name and write a different digit. On our second turn do the same for the second name. Every particular calculation finishes: long division gives the needed finite beginning, and its returning remainders reveal the zero-tail cases with a second, nine-tail name.
    🔗
    By our millionth turn, the millionth name will have a disagreement on the page. That turn is distant, but only finitely many moves precede it, all finite. The earlier disagreements have survived. Every name eventually gets its own turn, and so none can be the name of our completed number. We have another winning strategy for irrationality. This one relies on a scheduled escape from individual targets, rather than a special feature of repetition.
    🔗
    René Baire’s idea lets us escape targets containing many possibilities at once. Baire studied where continuity can fail: exceptional sets whose arrangement can matter more than their size as a count of points. His work in the late 1890s developed a way to describe smallness through the room remaining around such sets.
     2 
    Baire’s 1900 paper, “Nouvelle démonstration d’un théorème sur les fonctions discontinues,” returns to questions about continuity studied in his 1899 thesis. The decimal game is a later presentation of the category method.
    🔗
    René Baire (1874–1932)
    🔗
    Give the first player the event “no seven ever appears.” From any beginning, the second player can append seven. Every paper in the new drawer contains that seven, so none satisfies the event. If a seven was there already, any further move preserves the defeat. The event allows vastly many decimals, but it leaves us this way out from every drawer.
    🔗
    An event is nowhere dense when, from any finite beginning, we can append a finite block after which every continuation avoids it. A whole smaller drawer is clear. This is stronger than finding one possible safe paper. With only one safe paper, we might hand the pencil to our opponent and watch them choose a different continuation. With a safe drawer, all their choices stay safe. Our escape lasts beyond our turn.
    🔗
    Equality to one specified real is such an event. If the real begins \(0.2531415\ldots\) and the shared page says \(0.25\text{,}\) writing seven excludes it. If the page already says \(0.31\text{,}\) it is excluded before we start. In general a real has at most two decimal names, so at most two of the ten immediate subdrawers can contain its papers. There is always another subdrawer to choose.
    🔗
    That last argument did not require us to know the real’s digits. It proves that some safe labels exist, even when we cannot work out which they are. For one half, finding a safe label was an ordinary finite calculation. For an arbitrary real, it may not be. Keep the difference in view: room to move and instructions for finding the move are separate gifts.
    🔗
    Now change the event to “only finitely many sevens appear.” One seven will not defeat it. A million sevens will not defeat it either; we might have supplied a particularly tedious finite collection of sevens, followed by zeros forever. There is no finite block that settles the matter in our favor. Yet writing one seven on every turn plainly wins against this event. Baire’s next step describes exactly how such a sustained escape works.
    🔗
    Break the event into these possibilities:
    🔗
    \(F_1\text{:}\) there is no seven after position one.
    🔗
    \(F_2\text{:}\) there is no seven after position two.
    🔗
    \(F_3\text{:}\) there is no seven after position three.
    🔗
    Continue with every positive integer.
    🔗
    🔗
    If the whole decimal has only finitely many sevens, choose a position beyond its last seven; the corresponding \(F_n\) holds. If it has no sevens, all the \(F_n\) hold. Conversely, if any \(F_n\) holds, every seven is confined to its first \(n\) positions, so there can be only finitely many. The original event happens exactly when at least one entry on this list happens.
    🔗
    Each entry is nowhere dense. To defeat \(F_n\text{,}\) continue far enough to pass position \(n\) and write seven. Whatever follows, a seven now lies beyond the alleged boundary. We can defeat \(F_1\) on one turn, \(F_2\) on another, and continue. One turn cannot abolish the possibility of a last seven. The sequence of turns can prevent any seven from being the last.
    🔗
    A countable union of nowhere-dense events is called meager. “Countable” gives the pieces numbered places; “union” means that at least one of them happens. Finite lists are allowed, and pieces may overlap. Our event of finitely many sevens is meager, though it remains possible after every finite beginning: fill the remaining places with zeros. Meagerness does not say that an event is impossible, or attach a numerical probability to it. It says its ways of happening can be put into a countable list of pieces that individually leave room for escape.
    🔗
    For the proof, put the opponent’s pencil aside for a moment. Take any meager event, with pieces \(F_1,F_2,F_3,\ldots\text{,}\) and any starting drawer, perhaps \([0.21]\text{.}\) Because \(F_1\) is nowhere dense, append a nonempty finite block entering a drawer clear of \(F_1\text{.}\) From there choose a further drawer clear of \(F_2\text{.}\) It is still inside the first safe drawer, so the escape from \(F_1\) has not been lost. Continue with \(F_3\text{,}\) and then the next piece.
    🔗
    With our seven events, successive choices could look like this:
    🔗
    stage beginning after the choice targets secured so far
    1 \(0.217\) \(F_1\)
    2 \(0.217047\) \(F_1,F_2\)
    3 \(0.217047387\) \(F_1,F_2,F_3\)
    The last seven in each line lies beyond the position named by that stage’s target. In fact the first seven, at position three, already defeats \(F_2\) as well as \(F_1\text{.}\) The schedule need not refuse such good fortune; it merely ensures no entry is forgotten. For other events the chosen blocks will be different. What matters is that each new line extends the whole previous line and secures its assigned escape.
    🔗
    Each stage adds at least one digit, so these beginnings fit together into one endless paper. Choose any entry \(F_n\) from the list. Stage \(n\) put the paper in a drawer entirely clear of that entry, and every later stage stayed there. Hence the whole paper avoids \(F_n\text{.}\) Since this works for any entry we choose, the paper avoids all the pieces and their union. We do not need a last stage that certifies infinitely many escapes. Each possible accusation already has its own finite stage at which it was ruled out.
    🔗
    This is the Baire category theorem in the form our decimals need:
    🔗
    No meager event fills a drawer. After any finite beginning, there is a complete continuation outside it.
    🔗
    🔗
    For events concerning real values, each target includes all allowed names of the value. An all-nines continuation is still one of the papers we have kept inside our safe drawers. Shared endpoints do not open a hidden way back into an excluded event.
    🔗
    Now give the opponent the pencil again. After our escape from \(F_1\text{,}\) they append \(999999\text{.}\) Our page, formerly \(0.217\text{,}\) becomes \(0.217999999\text{.}\) Does the next escape still exist? Yes: \(F_2\) is nowhere dense in every drawer, including the drawer this new block has chosen. It supplies a further safe subdrawer. All earlier escapes remain intact. The opponent can make the next move start somewhere else, but cannot make it start somewhere with no way out.
    🔗
    This gives a winning strategy for the second player whenever the first player’s event is meager. A strategy supplies a legal finite move for every history that could lead to the player’s turn, including how the earlier digits were divided into moves. At our \(n\)th turn, escape \(F_n\) from the current drawer. The proof applies to every legal opposing play, not merely the particular reply \(999999\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If several blocks work, choose the first in a fixed ordering: shortest length first, then alphabetical order among equal lengths. The nonempty collection of successful blocks has a first member, so this specifies one reply. But a worker cannot necessarily recognize which blocks succeed. A mathematical description of a strategy need not be a program for carrying it out. We have retained the distinction that arose when we excluded an arbitrary real.
    🔗
    The theorem already tells us that the reals cannot all fit on a numbered list. Suppose someone hands us such a list. Let \(F_1\) say that the completed number equals the first entry, \(F_2\) that it equals the second, and so on. Equality to a specified real is nowhere dense, so the event “our number occurs on the list” is meager. Baire’s construction escapes it. The starting drawer could have any finite label: even after a thousand agreed digits, a real missing from the list remains available.
    🔗
    This is uncountability. The list may contain fractions, irrationals, familiar constants, or numbers whose digits nobody can calculate. Every entry still occupies at most two papers, and every turn still leaves a whole drawer free of its target. The second player can force a result outside the list while an opponent supplies all the intervening blocks. A list has given each of its entries a number; we use those numbers to arrange its defeat.
    🔗
    The first player’s opening move can confer an advantage too. Suppose there is a drawer in which the ways for their desired event to fail form a meager collection. They enter that drawer immediately. On each later turn they escape another nowhere-dense piece of the failure event. The opponent cannot leave the starting drawer or undo earlier escapes. The finished play avoids every listed way of failing and therefore satisfies the first player’s event.
    🔗
    Such an event is called comeager in that drawer: its complement there, the event of failure, is meager. For “infinitely many sevens appear,” the failure event is exactly the one we have just analyzed, “only finitely many sevens appear.” Thus infinitely many sevens is comeager in every drawer. Either player, if given the job of ensuring it, could write a seven on every turn. No particular seven guarantees victory; the rule guarantees that no seven is the last one we contribute.
    🔗
    There are two other descriptions worth attaching to these familiar possibilities. The event “a seven appears” is open: whenever it happens, a finite beginning eventually shows enough to guarantee it. The first seven is the witness. “Infinitely many sevens appear” is not open. No finite collection of sevens guarantees another one.
    🔗
    An event is dense when it remains possible in every drawer. Infinitely many sevens is dense: we can continue with sevens. Finitely many sevens is dense too: we can continue with zeros. These incompatible events both remain possible after every finite move, although a player can force the first by returning with another seven. Possibility alone says little about who controls the outcome.
    🔗
    When an event is both open and dense, something stronger is available on a single turn. Density supplies a successful continuation in the current drawer. Openness says some finite part of that continuation already guarantees success. Append that part, and the entire new drawer satisfies the event. We will meet this kind of opportunity again near the end of the book: a requirement may be reachable from wherever the work has got to, and a finite extension may secure it.
    🔗
    For our finite-block game, the winning conditions can be stated exactly. The second player has a winning strategy precisely when the first player’s event is meager. The first player has one precisely when their event is comeager in some drawer. We have constructed the strategies in these two cases. The game excursion in Appendix D proves the reverse directions. Writing a finite block on a turn is essential: the safe drawer may lie several digits away, and the player needs to reach it before the opponent takes over.
    🔗
    In the Scottish Book, Banach recorded an affirmative answer to Mazur’s question, dated August 4, 1935. This family of games is now called the Banach–Mazur game. The link to Baire’s smallness is the one we have followed: one player keeps making finite escapes, and the completed play avoids the countable collection. The wine was offered for a question whose answer concerns every possible reply, including replies long enough for the coffee to go cold.
    🔗
    Must one of the players always have a winning strategy? A game with such a strategy for one side is called determined. Every completed play has a winner by our rules; that does not yet put a way of ensuring victory into either player’s hands. We will leave that further question here. There is a difficulty closer to our worker’s desk, even in a game whose winning strategy we already know exists.
    🔗
    Let the first player now want the event “the resulting real is computable.” There are countably many finite sheets, and therefore countably many reals their productive printers name. Include every allowed decimal name of each such real. Equality to each one is nowhere dense, so computability is a meager event. The second player has a winning strategy: they can force the decimal to escape every finite printer, including our growing-gap sheet and every long-division sheet.
    🔗
    Try writing that strategy down for the human computer. For fractions, we could calculate a target’s next digit and disagree. The next program in our inventory writes six digits and falls silent. Its worker has not stopped moving. Perhaps the seventh digit will arrive tomorrow; perhaps the instructions send the worker searching forever. Our turn must end, and waiting forever is not a legal move. A fixed deadline does not solve it either: the digit might appear one step after the deadline. The inventory contains all program texts, not just the sheets that keep their promise to print a decimal.
    🔗
    Perhaps a cleverer strategy would avoid the waiting. Suppose there were any finite program carrying out a winning strategy for the second player. Give it an opponent with the simplest possible habit: write one zero on every turn. A single human computer can now play both sides. Write zero. Follow the strategy until its finite reply is finished. Write another zero. Follow the strategy again. The strategy must produce a legal reply on each turn; otherwise it was not the program we supposed it to be.
    🔗
    These combined directions form a finite instruction sheet. Every turn ends, every turn adds digits, and none of the digits is erased. So the sheet prints a complete decimal. Its real is computable. But this is a play the supposed winning strategy guaranteed would be noncomputable. The contradiction rules out the program. An opponent contributing nothing but zeros has exposed the limit of every proposed mechanical strategy, an unusually economical performance.
    🔗
    The winning strategy still exists. We proved there was a safe reply from every relevant drawer and a mathematical way to select one. What cannot exist is a finite sheet that finds those replies in every case. Our first escape from fractions could be performed by the worker; this escape from all printers includes the worker’s own possible instructions among the things to be defeated.
    🔗
    After any finite beginning, both computability and noncomputability remain possible events. Zeros give a computable continuation. Baire’s argument gives a noncomputable one. However impressive a finite page becomes, it has not decided which kind of future it has.
    🔗
    Six digits are still sitting beside the silent program. We could wait longer, but the additional silence would only enlarge the same uncertainty. Another possibility is to read the instructions themselves. Our worker has been given numbers as input; next we will give the worker a program, written out on the paper, and ask what it will do.
