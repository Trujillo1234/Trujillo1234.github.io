    Chapter 5 Proof on paper

    Watching the paper cannot always tell us whether another digit will come. A proof could tell us. The familiar jump back to itself can be dismissed in a sentence: it returns to the same instruction, so it will never reach anything else. We know more from that sentence than from watching a thousand jumps. For a less obliging program, the argument might occupy a hundred pages. If those pages finally settle the question, we need not wait for the calculation to settle it.
    🔗
    Our worker, however, must be able to recognize what the pages establish. Put a long division beside an explanation of its answer. In the calculation, a misplaced carry has a location. In the explanation, an omitted step may be hiding in “clearly.” A reader can sometimes repair it. Our worker has no instructions for being that kind of reader. Whatever licenses the next line must be available on the paper, in a form the worker can check.
    🔗
    David Hilbert put this demand at the centre of his study of mathematics’ foundations. A formal proof is a finite piece of writing governed by exact rules. Finding it can take imagination; checking it need not repeat the discovery. The person checking could be unimpressed by the theorem, misunderstand its importance, and still perform every check correctly. Insight has done its work and left something behind.
    🔗
    This gives our programs a new object to read. It also gives them something to react to. Imagine directions that say: look for a proof that this program cannot return 314; if one appears, write 314 and stop. Kleene’s construction can arrange that “this program” means the complete sheet doing the searching and writing. The proof would trigger the event it excludes. Before deciding whether that can happen, we must give the worker something more precise to recognize than an emphatic use of the word “cannot.”
    🔗
    The three instructions from the last chapter supply a small completed computation to work with. Write 7, move right, halt. Its trace records the configuration at each instruction boundary:
    🔗
    Entry Next instruction Card \(A\) Marked squares
    0 Write(A,7) \((0,0,0)\) none
    1 Move(A,0,1,0) \((0,0,0)\) 7 at \((0,0,0)\)
    2 Halt \((0,1,0)\) 7 at \((0,0,0)\)
    Call the record \(r\text{.}\) A checker can start at its first entry, compare each pair of neighbouring entries, and see that the last one reaches the halt. The move must leave the 7 at its old address. A record that carries the 7 along with the card has recorded a different action, however plausible its final answer looks.
    🔗
    For this program, “it halts” has a witness on the desk: \(r\text{.}\) For the program searching for a proof, the same kind of claim asks for a record of unknown length. We need to say that there is a record, that its beginning is right and its successive changes are right, and perhaps that there is not any such record. Our proof language will grow from these needs. It must let us describe a completed calculation without first having completed it.
    🔗
    The records themselves have many pieces: marks, addresses, place cards, configurations, and the positions of those configurations in a trace. We will collect pieces in sets. At first, picture a bag with objects as members, some of which may be other bags. There is no left-hand end, and putting an object in twice does not make two copies of it. This will shortly be an inconvenience. For now, the useful fact is that we can put several things together and talk about what is in the resulting collection.
    🔗
    Write \(a\in b\) when the object named \(a\) is a member of the object named \(b\text{.}\) If we know \(a\in b\) and also \(b\in c\text{,}\) we can write both facts on one line:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a\in b\qquad b\in c} {(a\in b)\land(b\in c)}. \end{equation*}
    The sign \(\land\) means “and.” Above the bar are the two facts available; below it is the statement we may infer. Such an arrangement is a proof tree. Its branches record the reasons for what appears beneath them. We can also take either part of an “and” statement back out and use it by itself.
    🔗
    Notice what this tree has not done. It has not concluded \(a\in c\text{.}\) Something inside an inner bag need not be a direct member of the outer bag. The rule only preserves the two statements we supplied. A checker can verify that preservation by comparing the writing, without deciding what any of the bags contain. With \(A\) and \(B\) standing for whole statements, its general form is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{A\qquad B}{A\land B}. \end{equation*}
    The statements could be about membership, or about the first two entries in \(r\text{.}\) A longer statement makes more writing to compare but does not change the rule.
    🔗
    We need rules for the experiments we make inside an argument too. Suppose \(e\) is empty and \(e\in b\text{.}\) Could \(b\) itself be \(e\text{?}\) Try that equality. Substituting \(e\) for \(b\) in our membership statement gives \(e\in e\text{.}\) But nothing belongs to \(e\text{.}\) The attempted equality has brought us to a statement and its negation, so we reject it.
    🔗
    The sign \(\neg\) means “not,” and \(\bot\) records a contradiction. We can leave the failed trial visible while marking where it begins and ends:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{l} \text{Available: }e\in b\text{ and }\neg(e\in e).\\[3pt] [b=e]_4\quad\text{temporary assumption}\\ \quad e\in e\quad\text{replace equals in }e\in b\\ \quad\bot\quad\text{contradiction}\\ \neg(b=e)\quad\text{close assumption 4}. \end{array} \end{equation*}
    The label 4 is there so the checker can identify the assumption being closed. We say it has been discharged. The facts \(e\in b\) and \(\neg(e\in e)\) are still needed. The trial equality is no longer a condition on our conclusion.
    🔗
    Nor can we keep using \(e\in e\text{,}\) although it remains plainly visible on the page. That line depended on the equality we tried. If failed arguments were allowed to leave their consequences available for later use, mathematics would accumulate results at an impressive rate. The checker must therefore keep track of the assumptions behind each line, removing only the ones a rule permits it to discharge.
    🔗
    We also allow the classical version of contradiction: assume \(\neg A\text{,}\) derive a contradiction, and conclude \(A\text{.}\) This is reductio, or proof by contradiction. Equality gives us two further familiar permissions: an object equals itself, and equals may replace one another in a statement. We have just used the second permission to expose the failed equality.
    🔗
    An existence claim changes what we may write in a different way. From \(a\in b\text{,}\) we may say that \(b\) has a member:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a\in b}{\exists x(x\in b)}. \end{equation*}
    Read \(\exists x\) as “there exists an \(x\text{.}\)” The object \(a\) is our example. The letter \(x\) shows the place in the condition that the example fills; it is not a newly discovered object named \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Coming back in the other direction requires care. Suppose someone only tells us that \(b\) has a member. We may give an unknown member a temporary name, \(u\text{,}\) and reason from \(u\in b\text{.}\) We cannot choose a familiar object already on the desk and decide that it must be the promised member. If we knew that particular object was outside \(b\text{,}\) our choice would manufacture a contradiction where there was none. The bag could have had a different member all along.
    🔗
    The temporary name must be fresh, and anything carried out of its branch must be independent of which member it names. This condition sounds abstract until we try a whole proof. Assume that \(b\) has a member and \(e\) has none. Call these starting claims \(S\) and \(N\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} S:\ \exists x(x\in b),\qquad N:\ \forall z\neg(z\in e). \end{equation*}
    The sign \(\forall z\) means “for every \(z\)”; here it says that every object fails to belong to \(e\text{.}\) Name a member of \(b\) temporarily, then try making \(b\) and \(e\) equal:
    🔗
    Line What is written Why it may be written
    1 \(\exists x(x\in b)\) Starting assumption \(S\)
    2 \(\forall z\neg(z\in e)\) Starting assumption \(N\)
    3 \([u\in b]_7\) Name a member promised by line 1
    4 \([b=e]_8\) Try equality
    5 \(u\in e\) Replace \(b\) by \(e\) in line 3
    6 \(\neg(u\in e)\) Use line 2 for \(u\)
    7 \(\bot\) Lines 5 and 6 contradict
    8 \(\neg(b=e)\) Close assumption 8
    9 \(\neg(b=e)\) Close witness assumption 7
    At line 8 the attempted equality has failed. We are still inside the branch where \(u\) names the unknown member. At line 9 we can leave that branch because the conclusion does not mention \(u\text{.}\) Whichever member \(b\) has, its existence prevents \(b\) from being the empty object \(e\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The last two lines say the same thing, but do not do the same work. Line 5 depends on assumptions 7 and 8; line 6 depends on \(N\text{.}\) Their contradiction therefore uses \(N,7,8\text{.}\) Closing 8 leaves \(N,7\text{.}\) Closing the witness branch replaces 7 with the existence claim \(S\) that allowed us to open it. We finish with exactly \(S,N\text{,}\) our original assumptions. No particular member has been selected outside the argument.
    🔗
    Try carrying \(u\in b\) out instead. The checker refuses: \(u\) is still in the proposed conclusion. The name was borrowed for the branch, and the conclusion is trying to keep it. In general the fresh name must be absent from the original existence statement, the conclusion carried out, and the other assumptions left open. All three conditions can be checked by looking through finite writing. A proof beginning with the claim “there is a completed record ending in 314” could likewise name the promised record temporarily and investigate its contents. That name would let the argument proceed. It would not perform the calculation or put the three digits on our worker’s answer row.
    🔗
    Even replacing a name can change a statement if we ignore its surroundings. In \(\exists x(x\in b)\text{,}\) the \(x\)’s are bound by “there exists”; \(b\) is free to name the bag. Suppose we want to use \(x\) as another name for that bag. Blindly replacing \(b\) produces \(\exists x(x\in x)\text{,}\) a claim that something belongs to itself. The name has been caught by the wrong job. First rename the bound letter, giving \(\exists z(z\in b)\text{.}\) Then the replacement gives \(\exists z(z\in x)\text{,}\) still the claim that our bag has a member. This is called avoiding capture. Renaming and checking where a letter is bound are more tasks for the worker’s pencil, not appeals to insight.
    🔗
    Our decimal arguments already used two other familiar forms of reasoning. We divided the proposed repeating blocks into cases: those containing a 1 and those containing none. We also took an arbitrary denominator and examined its possible remainders. Both forms have a place among these rules.
    🔗
    For cases, suppose \(b\) contains \(a\) or contains \(c\text{.}\) In the first branch, \(a\) witnesses that \(b\) has a member; in the second, \(c\) witnesses it. Either way we reach the same existence statement. We need not find out which branch describes the bag. The checker requires both branches to reach that conclusion and keeps any other assumptions each branch used.
    🔗
    For an arbitrary object, the restriction is different. We may prove a property of an object without using anything special about it, then conclude that every object has the property. If an open assumption says \(a\in b\text{,}\) the particular name \(a\) is already doing special work. We cannot use that assumption to conclude that everything belongs to \(b\text{.}\) The name chosen for an arbitrary object must be absent from the assumptions still open.
    🔗
    We can assemble these larger moves from a small stock of signs. “If \(A\text{,}\) then \(B\)” rules out \(A\) together with the failure of \(B\text{.}\) “\(A\) or \(B\)” rules out their both failing. “Every \(x\) has property \(A\)” rules out an example lacking it:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} A\to B&:=\neg(A\land\neg B),\\ A\lor B&:=\neg(\neg A\land\neg B),\\ \forall x\,A(x)&:=\neg\exists x\,\neg A(x). \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    For example, if a derivation takes us from an assumed \(A\) to \(B\text{,}\) anyone offering \(A\land\neg B\) supplies both what starts that derivation and what contradicts its ending. The negation rule closes the attempt and gives the first displayed formula. Once this implication and \(A\) are available, classical contradiction gives \(B\text{.}\) The “or” allows both alternatives to hold; a bag containing both \(a\) and \(c\) still has a member.
    🔗
    Appendix B follows a few of these arguments with all their assumptions left visible. The full collection of proof cards appears in Chapter 2 of the Technical Companion. Their basic logical signs are \(\land,\neg,\exists\text{,}\) with membership, equality, and the contradiction marker. The abbreviations let us use familiar forms of reasoning without asking the checker to acquire a separate faculty for each one.
    🔗
    We can now say exactly which sets our proof method promises. For collecting finite records we need an empty collection, and the ability to add one object to a collection already available. Adding that object is called adjunction. These two promises are Adjunctive Set Theory, or AS. Its first axiom says that an empty object exists:
    \begin{equation*} \exists e\,\forall z\,\neg(z\in e). \tag{Empty} \end{equation*}
    The second says that, given \(x\) and \(a\text{,}\) there is a \(b\) containing precisely the old members of \(x\) together with \(a\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \forall x\,\forall a\,\exists b\,\forall z\, \bigl(z\in b\leftrightarrow(z\in x\lor z=a)\bigr). \tag{Adjunction} \end{equation*}
    The sign \(\leftrightarrow\) means “if and only if,” two implications joined by “and.” The left-to-right implication keeps uninvited objects out; the right-to-left one puts the specified objects in.
    🔗
    Choose an empty object \(e\) and adjoin \(e\) to it, obtaining \(b\text{.}\) To see that \(e\in b\text{,}\) use the membership description for the particular object \(e\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} e\in b\leftrightarrow(e\in e\lor e=e). \end{equation*}
    The equality \(e=e\) gives the right-hand alternative, which gives the disjunction. The implication then gives the membership we wanted. These moves fit together as a proof tree:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{gathered} \frac{ (e\in e\lor e=e)\to e\in b \qquad \dfrac{e=e}{e\in e\lor e=e}\ \text{or} }{e\in b}\\[3pt]\text{use if}. \end{gathered} \end{equation*}
    Emptiness gives \(\neg(e\in e)\text{.}\) Our earlier contradiction argument therefore shows that \(b\ne e\text{.}\) We have made two objects whose difference is provable from the axioms. Adjoin \(b\) to \(b\text{,}\) obtaining \(c\text{.}\) Its old member \(e\) remains, and its new member \(b\) joins it:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{e\in c\qquad b\in c} {(e\in c)\land(b\in c)}. \end{equation*}
    The old-member part of Adjunction supplies the first premise; the new-member part, with \(b=b\text{,}\) supplies the second. The rule for “and” has not changed since our first proof tree.
    🔗
    We can also start with an arbitrary object \(a\) and adjoin it to an empty object. The resulting collection contains \(a\text{,}\) so it supplies an example of a set to which \(a\) belongs. Close the temporary witness names for that collection and for the empty object. No open assumption then singles out \(a\text{,}\) and the arbitrary-object rule gives
    \begin{equation*} \forall a\,\exists y\,(a\in y). \end{equation*}
    Every object belongs to some object. Nothing here promises one enormous object containing everything. Each \(a\) may have its own suitable \(y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Nor do the two axioms say that collections with the same members must be the same object. That further principle is extensionality. We have not included it. We have also not assumed a completed infinite set. The axioms let us make the particular finite collections our records need, while leaving open what else a world satisfying them may contain.
    🔗
    Now try to preserve the word 707 in a set. Put 7 in, put 0 in, put 7 in again. We have lost both the order and the second occurrence of 7. The bag is being a perfectly good set and a poor notebook. Attach the position to each digit before collecting them instead:
    \begin{equation*} (0,7),\qquad(1,0),\qquad(2,7). \end{equation*}
    Each ordered pair has a first item, the position, and a second item, the mark. The two sevens now occur in different pairs. But if our only containers are unordered sets, we must explain how a pair keeps its first item first.
    🔗
    For two objects \(a,b\text{,}\) make a container \(u\) with just \(a\text{,}\) a container \(v\) with \(a,b\text{,}\) and a container \(p\) with those two containers:
    \begin{equation*} u=\{a\},\qquad v=\{a,b\},\qquad p=\{u,v\}. \end{equation*}
    Empty and Adjunction supply the containers. The braces specify their members; they do not assert that there is only one object with those members. Open the inner containers of \(p\text{.}\) The object common to all of them is \(a\text{,}\) because the container \(u\) has no other member. Any other object that appears is \(b\text{.}\) If no other object appears, then \(b=a\text{.}\) The first and second items can therefore be recovered.
    🔗
    For \((0,7)\text{,}\) the inner contents are \(\{0\}\) and \(\{0,7\}\text{:}\) 0 is common, and 7 is the other item. For \((7,0)\text{,}\) they are \(\{7\}\) and \(\{7,0\}\text{,}\) recovering 7 first. For \((7,7)\text{,}\) only 7 appears in either inner container, recovering it twice. Distinct containers with identical contents cause no confusion about these recovered items. We need a reliable representation of the pair, not a unique physical bag for it.
    🔗
    Collecting the three positioned digits now preserves 707. We can treat a configuration similarly: join each mark to its address, each card name to its address, and the next instruction to its designated place. Give the configurations positions in the trace as well. The sequence of sequences can be recovered from the nested collections.
    🔗
    One check from \(r\) can be written out so that we can see what these containers are doing. In entry 1, let \(m\) name the collection of marked-square entries, \(a\) the address \((0,0,0)\text{,}\) and \(s\) the code for 7. The assertion that the required address–mark pair occurs in \(m\) is
    \begin{equation*} \begin{split} \exists t\exists u\exists v\,\bigl(&t\in m\ \land\\ &\forall z(z\in u\leftrightarrow z=a)\ \land\\ &\forall z(z\in v\leftrightarrow(z=a\lor z=s))\ \land\\ &\forall z(z\in t\leftrightarrow(z=u\lor z=v))\bigr). \end{split} \end{equation*}
    Read upward from the inner containers if that is easier. The second line gives \(u\) just the address \(a\text{;}\) the third gives \(v\) the address and the mark. The last line puts \(u,v\) into \(t\text{,}\) making the pair code. The first line puts \(t\) into the marked-square collection \(m\text{.}\) Construct these objects by adjunction, and take \(m\) to contain the resulting \(t\text{.}\) They supply the examples for the three “there exists” signs and a proof of the displayed claim.
    🔗
    A record with a 9 instead of a 7 fails the comparison with the distinct code for 7. A record with the correct mark but a moved address can fail a different check. We must also check the next instruction and every part that was supposed to remain unchanged. The statement above certifies one piece of a transition. It cannot excuse errors in the rest.
    🔗
    There is a further step between this example and a language for all computation records. We can build each particular finite record from finitely many containers. Our searcher needs one fixed description of what counts as a record, however long it is, and uniform ways of reading its entries. The small construction does not establish that larger result.
    🔗
    Here we use a coding theorem. Zlatan Damnjanovic proved that adjunctive set theory, with or without extensionality, and Robinson arithmetic, a weak arithmetic theory, can represent each other. Albert Visser’s work supplies the sequence-coding interpretation used for our records.
     1 
    Damnjanovic, “Mutual Interpretability of Robinson Arithmetic and Adjunctive Set Theory with Extensionality,” Bulletin of Symbolic Logic 23 (2017), 381–404, doi:10.1017/bsl.2017.30. Chapter 2 of the Technical Companion specifies the arithmetic and sequence properties used here and gives Visser’s references. The full interpretation is not constructed in this book.
    🔗
    An interpretation supplies formulas saying which objects represent numbers and records, how to read their entries, and when different codes represent the same thing. Recall what went wrong with 707: membership alone gave no positions. The interpreted arithmetic and sequence laws supply the required structure uniformly, with proofs in AS. We continue to use Empty and Adjunction as our axioms; the representation lets those axioms talk about more than their short statements suggest.
    🔗
    Fix that representation, including the formulas for instructions and output words. It has a property we will use immediately: every actual successful finite computation can be converted mechanically into a proof of its output sentence. We call this the finite-trace property. For \(r\text{,}\) the individual checks we have been making become pieces of such a proof. A longer completed record takes more pieces, but the conversion finishes for each record. The interpretation justifies this uniform conversion; our three-entry illustration alone did not.
    🔗
    Suppose our waiting program found a proof excluding 314, then responded by writing those digits and stopping. Its search up to that proof, its checking, and its final writes would make one completed finite computation. The finite-trace property would turn that computation into a proof that it returned 314. The response would therefore furnish a second proof, contradicting the very claim that set it going. We now have the means to make the program’s provocation exact.
    🔗
    The waiting program wants to use proofs without waiting for a mathematician to invent them. It can do so in the most extravagant way imaginable: try every finite piece of writing. Order the strings first by length and then alphabetically. With a two-symbol alphabet the list begins
    \begin{equation*} 0,\ 1;\qquad00,\ 01,\ 10,\ 11;\qquad 000,\ 001,\ 010,\ldots. \end{equation*}
    Use our proof alphabet in place of the two symbols. Names such as v0 and v17 provide indefinitely many variables using finitely many marks. Any particular finite proof has a place in this list, with only finitely many strings before it.
    🔗
    For each candidate, the checker works from the leaves of the proposed tree toward its conclusion. It matches the rule at each step, checks substitutions and fresh names, and carries the remaining assumptions forward. From \(e\in c\) and \(b\in c\text{,}\) the conclusion \((e\in c)\land(c\in b)\) fails: the second pair of names has been reversed. Intending the right statement cannot make the written step pass.
    🔗
    Most candidates are not even grammatical. Of the grammatical ones, most prove nothing. The search goes on with a dedication no writer should emulate. Each check finishes, and every valid finite proof eventually gets checked. If the desired conclusion has a proof, the search finds it. If it has none, the search continues. Looking for both a statement and its negation does not guarantee an answer; both searches can remain unsuccessful.
    🔗
    We can allow more axioms than the two we have printed, including an endless list produced by a program. A proof using an axiom from that list attaches a finite record of the axiom-printer reaching it. The checker examines the attachment rather than waiting to see what the printer might produce in the future. Thus each candidate still receives a finite check, and every genuine certificate remains eventually available to the search.
    🔗
    Our contrary program can now have exact instructions. Call the chosen proof method \(T\text{.}\) Before constructing the program, fix how a claim about an output is written. Given program text \(e\) and a finite digit word \(w\text{,}\) the sentence-making procedure produces \(\operatorname{Out}(e,w)\text{:}\) there is a correct completed record of that program ending with that word. The procedure must finish and must describe ordinary finite records correctly. Its written form must also let the searcher recover \(w\) from a matching conclusion. Recognizing a claim about 314 has to reveal the three digits to be copied.
    🔗
    For the argument, \(T\) must use our classical first-order proof rules, its certificates must be mechanically enumerable, and the fixed output sentences must have the finite-trace property in \(T\text{.}\) Assume also that \(T\) is consistent: it never proves both a statement and its negation. AS with the fixed interpretation is our intended example. These are actual requirements, not consequences of calling a method consistent. A consistent collection of writing that has no way to describe computations would give this searcher nothing suitable to recognize.
    🔗
    Let the program search for a proof of \(\neg\operatorname{Out}(e,w)\text{,}\) for any finite digit word \(w\text{.}\) When it finds one, it copies \(w\) to its result and halts. Kleene supplies its own complete description in place of \(e\text{.}\) With SELF naming that description, the task has this short form:
    🔗
    Search: Call NextProof(proof)
Call ExcludedWord(proof, SELF, answer, word)
If(answer,N) Goto Search
Call Copy(word, RESULT)
Halt
    NextProof continues the enumeration until another checked proof appears. ExcludedWord looks for the specified negative output claim about SELF. A match puts Y in answer and the excluded word in word; otherwise it puts N in answer. The program recognizes a written pattern. It is not required to agree with what the pattern says.
    🔗
    Call this complete program \(E\text{.}\) Suppose a matching proof exists. There is a first one in the search order; say it excludes 314. The program reaches it, recognizes the claim, copies 314, and stops. This is a particular finite computation, including all the searching needed to reach that proof. By the finite-trace property, it yields a \(T\)-proof of \(\operatorname{Out}(E,314)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    But the proof that prompted the response was a \(T\)-proof of \(\neg\operatorname{Out}(E,314)\text{.}\) We have obtained both a statement and its negation from a method assumed consistent. The supposed first proof therefore cannot exist. If it had excluded 0007 instead, the program would have copied all four symbols and supplied the corresponding contradiction. Here outputs are words: the zeros in 0007 are part of the promised response.
    🔗
    For every particular finite word \(w\text{,}\) we have shown
    \begin{equation*} T\text{ does not prove }\neg\operatorname{Out}(E,w). \end{equation*}
    In the ordinary execution, \(E\) searches forever. There is no matching certificate, and finding one is its only route to an output. Its result row stays blank. Yet \(T\) cannot exclude any specified finite result. It cannot rule out 314, or 0007, or the word we might choose tomorrow.
    🔗
    The conclusion depends on only one direction of trust between computation and proof. When a computation actually finishes, its finite record can be made into a proof. We have not assumed that every proof asserting a completed computation gives us an ordinary finite record that can actually be produced. Consistency forbids contradictory theorems. It does not, by itself, make all theorems about the worker true of the work at our desk.
    🔗
    Try adding \(\operatorname{Out}(E,314)\) to \(T\) as an extra assertion. If this caused a contradiction, take the contradicting proof and close the extra assumption by our negation rule. That would leave an old \(T\)-proof of \(\neg\operatorname{Out}(E,314)\text{.}\) We have just shown that no such proof exists. The new assertion can therefore be added consistently.
    🔗
    The same holds for any particular finite word, considered separately. Choosing 314 and choosing 0007 give different extensions of the proof method. Nothing in the argument permits us to demand both incompatible results at once. Later we will want something more carefully coordinated: a longer decimal beginning that keeps the shorter beginning already chosen. For now, even this single consistent assertion of 314 is strange enough. The program it describes is still searching.
    🔗
    We can isolate the failure of proof with an even smaller searcher. Let \(J\) look only for a proof saying that \(J\) never halts, and halt if it finds one. Kleene again ensures that the sentence concerns the complete program that responds. There is no other exit. If the proof existed, the program would reach it and halt; its completed trace would then supply a proof that it halts. Consistency excludes the pair, so the searched-for proof does not exist.
    🔗
    We can finish that argument from outside the search. With no proof to find and no other way to stop, \(J\) runs forever. The sentence it seeks is true and unprovable in \(T\text{.}\) This does not show, from consistency alone, that its opposite is unprovable. A consistent theory need not be correct about ordinary computations. Rosser’s refinement obtains a sentence with neither side provable under the appropriate hypotheses; the separate theorem about proving a theory’s own consistency is another result. Neither is needed for the consistent assertion about 314 that is now on our desk.
    🔗
    Gödel first revealed the incompleteness obstruction. Our route through Turing’s worker and Kleene’s self-description came later. Hilbert’s requirement that proof be exact enough to check has allowed a program to read a proof of its own behaviour, and Church’s world of expressions has supplied the means of making that behaviour answer back. The rules did not become loose. They became precise enough to support the construction that exposes their limit.
    🔗
    Leave \(E\) running. Beside it put the consistently enlarged theory, with its assertion that \(E\) returns 314. We know what a completed record would have to contain: the prescribed beginning, every instructed change, and the final writes. No ordinary completed record lies between the two pieces of paper. The argument has secured consistency without filling that gap. Henkin’s construction will begin by giving the promised record a name, and then build a world in which that name has something to name.
