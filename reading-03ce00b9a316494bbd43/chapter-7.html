    Chapter 7 Long division again

    The remainder is exactly where we left it. Bring down a zero, subtract the divisor, count the successful subtractions. The next digit appears. Our worker has no reason to change these directions after everything that has happened to us.
    🔗
    We have a reason to look at the dividend and divisor again. Until now they arrived on the page already written. Suppose two instruction sheets are to supply them: \(P\) prints the numerator, \(Q\) the denominator. We will fix those sheets before choosing the decimal we want. Then a suitable mathematical world will contain completed records of their work, with integer outputs whose quotient follows our chosen decimal at every ordinary finite place. The growing-gap decimal will do. The halting decimal will do too.
    🔗
    The two workers at our ordinary desk will go on searching forever. Henkin’s completed records will be longer than every ordinary stage of that search. This is where the different meanings of finite enter the division. No digit is being slipped past the worker, and no subtraction has received permission to misbehave.
    🔗
    Ask for \(314\text{.}\) The drawer runs from \(0.314\) to \(0.315\text{,}\) and we can put a fraction halfway inside it:
    \begin{equation*} 0.314+0.0005=0.3145=\frac{629}{2000}. \end{equation*}
    The requested digits occupy the first three places. The extra five keeps the fraction away from both ends of the drawer. Asking for \(003\) gives
    \begin{equation*} 0.003+0.0005=0.0035=\frac{7}{2000}. \end{equation*}
    The numerator has no leading zeros to remember. The denominator keeps their places for it.
    🔗
    Here is the same work for any finite request. Read its digits as an integer \(a\text{,}\) and let \(k\) count their places. Make the numerator \(2a+1\text{,}\) and make the denominator by writing a two followed by \(k\) zeros. Thus \(314\) gives \(2\cdot314+1=629\) above \(2000\text{.}\) The quotient is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2a+1}{2\cdot10^k} =\frac{a}{10^k}+\frac{1}{2\cdot10^k}. \end{equation*}
    The first term is the drawer’s lower end; the second moves halfway across its width. Addition, multiplication by two, and the writing of zeros are already within our worker’s reach. Long division produces the requested word, then a five, then zeros.
    🔗
    This works at the edges too. Three zeros give \(1/2000=0.0005\text{;}\) three nines give \(1999/2000=0.9995\text{.}\) Neither request has been excluded. A million requested digits would make an awkward pair of integers to carry home, but the directions would scarcely grow.
    🔗
    Our printers will get their request from a proof. They search for a certificate saying that their quotient cannot have a particular beginning, then prepare the fraction with exactly that beginning. An objection to \(314\) will supply \(314\) as the copying material. The objection is doing rather more of the work than it intended.
    🔗
    Keep the proof method \(T\) and the record coding from Chapter 5. We assume consistency, a mechanical enumeration of certificates, and the arithmetic and record-verification properties specified there and in Appendix B. In particular, an actual finite execution of this construction must give a \(T\)-proof of its whole checked record: the two printer runs, their integer values, and their division. Across models the coding must also retain the ordinary arithmetic laws and the deterministic changes from one configuration to the next. Those are the hypotheses of the result below. The adjunctive setting uses the interpreted coding described in the appendix; an arbitrary consistent collection of sentences would not supply these properties merely by being consistent.
    🔗
    Both printers follow the same search in the same order, from empty input. When it returns a word, \(P\) performs the numerator job and \(Q\) the denominator job. There remains the familiar difficulty: the search has to recognize sentences about these very printers.
    🔗
    Kleene’s copying construction gives us a controller with its own text available as input. Recall the white and blue sheets from Chapter 4. White built a package from supplied text. Blue supplied the white text and obeyed the package. The copying reconstructed blue’s description and handed it to the chosen task. Now the task will prepare a search about two printers.
    🔗
    Give that task a proposed controller text \(e\text{.}\) It writes two new descriptions, \(P_e\) and \(Q_e\text{:}\) obey \(e\text{,}\) take the word it returns, and do the appropriate numerator or denominator job. Writing these descriptions does not require waiting for \(e\) to finish. A sheet can say “obey this sheet” while the sheet it names is still quietly lying on the desk.
    🔗
    With \(P_e,Q_e\) written, the task can assemble sentences about their completed runs and the division of their results. It prepares a search for certificates excluding finite beginnings from that division, then obeys the search. Kleene supplies a controller \(e\) that carries out this task on its own description. The printers it describes begin by obeying this very controller. Copying has closed the loop.
    🔗
    Follow a possible answer through it. A worker obeying \(P_e\) first obeys the controller. The controller reconstructs the printer texts, builds their search, and follows it. Suppose the search finds an exclusion of \(003\text{.}\) It returns that word to the controller, which returns it to the waiting printer. The numerator job reads its value as three and calculates \(2\cdot3+1=7\text{.}\) The other printer’s identical search returns the same word; its denominator job counts three places and writes \(2000\text{.}\) Division gives \(0.0035\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We have followed the response without assuming that the search ever finds anything. Fix these two sheets now and call them \(P,Q\text{.}\) Their text depends on \(T\text{,}\) since the search contains its proof checker. No infinite decimal has yet been chosen.
    🔗
    For the request \(314\text{,}\) the numerator’s paper ends with \(629\) and the denominator’s with \(2000\text{.}\) We fasten their completed computations to a record of dividing those very outputs. The packet must keep the connection between a row of written marks and its integer value. Reading \(629\) from left to right gives
    \begin{equation*} 0\longrightarrow6\longrightarrow62\longrightarrow629: \end{equation*}
    at each arrow, multiply the previous value by ten and add the next digit. Reading \(2000\) gives \(0,2,20,200,2000\text{.}\) These evaluation chains are part of the checked packet. Let \(p,q\) be their last values. Require \(q>0\) and \(0\leq p<q\text{,}\) so the division starts with \(0.\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Here is the division component for our example:
    🔗
    place ten times old remainder next digit new remainder
    1 6290 3 290
    2 2900 1 900
    3 9000 4 1000
    4 10000 5 0
    At the second line, \(2900=1\cdot2000+900\text{.}\) At the third, \(9000=4\cdot2000+1000\text{.}\) Each remainder is nonnegative and smaller than \(2000\text{.}\) The fourth line reaches zero; we can stop saving the record there, although the decimal printer would continue writing zeros.
    🔗
    The original numerator is still visible in the cumulative checks:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 10\cdot629&=3\cdot2000+290,\\ 100\cdot629&=31\cdot2000+900,\\ 1000\cdot629&=314\cdot2000+1000,\\ 10000\cdot629&=3145\cdot2000+0. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    This is the division invariant from Chapter 2, now attached to the printer records. From the third line we recover
    \begin{equation*} 314\cdot2000<1000\cdot629<315\cdot2000, \end{equation*}
    or \(0.314<p/q<0.315\text{.}\) The same fraction meets the shorter requests \(3\) and \(31\text{,}\) and the saved division has their digits in its first and second places. No new fraction has entered between the checks.
    🔗
    Make this fastening part of the sentence we search for. For a finite word \(s\text{,}\) write
    \begin{equation*} \Phi_s=\exists d\, \bigl(\operatorname{Record}(d)\land\operatorname{Prefix}_s(d)\bigr). \end{equation*}
    Read this as “there is one checked packet \(d\) whose division begins with \(s\text{.}\)” The first abbreviation checks the completed printer runs, their evaluated outputs, and one division record ending at a digit boundary with remainder zero. The second checks the requested digits and the corresponding interval inequalities. It requires the record to contain those digit positions. Even a request for a thousand zeros demands a thousand recorded places; a single zero is not allowed to stand in for them.
    🔗
    The prefix checks accumulate. A request for \(3141\) keeps all the checks for \(314\) and adds the fourth place and its narrower interval. Our example fails this new request: its fourth digit is five. A packet that passes, however, automatically passes the earlier checks with the very same printer outputs. Every longer prefix keeps its shorter prefixes as conjuncts—statements joined by “and”—so the proof rule for taking a part recovers them. The appendix spells out these finite formulas.
    🔗
    The search lists finite words together with candidate certificates, checks each certificate, and compares its conclusion with the negation \(\neg\Phi_s\) prepared from the word. A match says precisely that no such common packet exists. The word beside the certificate becomes the printers’ request.
    🔗
    Let an exclusion certificate arrive, and take the first one in the search order. Suppose it excludes \(314\text{.}\) Both printers find it. They print \(629\) and \(2000\text{,}\) and division gives the packet we have just checked. Searching up to this certificate, checking it, returning the word, and carrying out the arithmetic are all ordinary finite work. Our verification hypothesis turns that completed work into a \(T\)-proof of \(\Phi_{314}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The certificate proves \(\neg\Phi_{314}\text{.}\) We have obtained both a statement and its negation from the same proof method. Consistency rules out the supposed certificate. Its digits made no difference: whichever word arrived first, the printers would construct a fraction inside that word’s drawer. Thus no ordinary finite word has an exclusion certificate.
    🔗
    We have built two integer printers and proved that neither prints its integer. This is a success with an unusually poor demonstration at the desk. Their answer rows remain blank while the searches continue.
    🔗
    For a particular word \(s\text{,}\) now add \(\Phi_s\) to the proof method. If the enlarged theory proved a contradiction, we could discharge this additional assumption and conclude \(\neg\Phi_s\) in \(T\text{.}\) That would be the forbidden certificate. Hence \(T+\Phi_s\) is consistent. Henkin supplies a model in which a suitable completed packet exists.
    🔗
    This gives a model for \(314\text{,}\) a model for \(3141\text{,}\) and a model for any other finite request. We still need all the compatible requests in one place. Changing models at each new digit would leave us with an endless succession of separate fractions.
    🔗
    Choose an entire sequence of digits. Perhaps it starts \(31415926\ldots\text{;}\) perhaps it answers our halting questions. Give the packet one new name, \(d\text{.}\) Henkin’s notebook is to contain \(T\text{,}\) the assertion that \(d\) is a checked record, and the demands
    🔗
    The division in \(d\) begins with \(3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The division in \(d\) begins with \(31\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The division in \(d\) begins with \(314\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The division in \(d\) begins with \(3141\text{.}\)
    🔗
    And so on, through every ordinary finite beginning of the chosen sequence.
    🔗
    🔗
    Take any finite selection of entries. Among its requested beginnings there is a longest one. We have just obtained a model with a packet for that request. Interpret \(d\) as that packet. Because its prefix checks retain all the earlier checks, the same packet meets every shorter request in our selection. It also meets the record requirements, and its model satisfies \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Every finite selection can therefore be satisfied. Compactness gives a model satisfying the entire notebook. Its one packet contains the two completed printer runs, one pair of integer values, and their division. At every ordinary finite position, that division displays the chosen digit.
    🔗
    Here is the order of the choices, collected into the result:
    🔗
    Fix a consistent, mechanically enumerable classical first-order theory \(T\text{,}\) together with coding that has the arithmetic, deterministic-record, and finite-verification properties described above. There are two fixed programs \(P,Q\) such that, for every external infinite decimal sequence, a suitable model of \(T\) contains one completed record of their integer outputs and their division, agreeing with that sequence at all ordinary finite positions.
    🔗
    🔗
    The sheets were fixed before the sequence was chosen. The model may depend on that sequence. It need not extend any particular model we already have.
    🔗
    The notebook’s requests come from outside. An uncomputable target supplies no mechanical recipe for writing all of them, and the target need not itself be an object in the resulting model. The existence argument can use its every finite beginning without turning the collection of beginnings into a program. Nothing here starts the printers on our desk producing answers.
    🔗
    The model’s packet has a last division line, where the remainder is zero. Try to put a finger on it. At the thousandth place? The notebook requires a record of the first thousand and one digits too. At the millionth? A longer demand reaches beyond that. Every ordinary place is followed by another requested place in the same record. Its last index, call it \(b\text{,}\) is larger than every ordinary numeral.
    🔗
    There is no first extra place waiting immediately after ordinary counting. If \(b-1\) were the millionth place, \(b\) would be a million and one, and our notebook already reaches farther. The same objection works for every ordinary numeral, and for \(b-2\) or \(b-3\) as well. Looking backward from the end does not bring us to a border we can cross with the next ordinary step.
    🔗
    Inside the model, \(p\) and \(q\) are integers, and their ratio is rational. The division has reached zero remainder at its last saved line; further steps would write zeros. Yet every ordinary position can follow
    \begin{equation*} 0.01001000100001000001\ldots. \end{equation*}
    Beyond any ordinary position there is another one, and beyond that one a longer gap. No ordinary position begins the final zero tail. The model’s endpoint and our ordinary positions belong to different ranges of the word finite.
    🔗
    The printer records also have nonstandard lengths. At each ordinary stage, the deterministic checks fix their configurations to be those of the searches at our desk. Neither ordinary search finishes. A completed internal record includes something that its model accepts as an exclusion certificate, but it cannot be an ordinary finite certificate we can take home and finish checking. Such a certificate would restart the contradiction above.
    🔗
    Now ask for nines. Our finite fractions start
    \begin{equation*} \frac{19}{20}=0.95,\qquad \frac{199}{200}=0.995,\qquad \frac{1999}{2000}=0.9995. \end{equation*}
    Their positive gaps below one are \(1/20,1/200,1/2000\text{.}\) Each new request narrows the gap without closing it.
    🔗
    Put every nine-request into the notebook, all attached to the same packet. In the model supplied by compactness, its quotient is still less than one, as the record requires. Yet it has a nine at every ordinary position. If \(h=1-p/q\) is its positive gap, the request for the first \(n+1\) nines gives
    \begin{equation*} 0<h\leq\frac{1}{10^{n+1}}<\frac{1}{10^n} \end{equation*}
    for every ordinary positive integer \(n\text{.}\) Its eventual zeros lie beyond all those positions.
    🔗
    Read these nines as an ordinary real decimal and they still name exactly one. The equality \(0.999\ldots=1\) has lost nothing. Two different ordinary real numbers have a positive distance between them, and some sufficiently fine decimal interval separates them. The positive \(h\) above belongs to the model’s ordered arithmetic; no ordinary decimal resolution separates its quotient from one. Agreement at every ordinary position has not identified an element of that larger ordered world.
    🔗
    Both names of an ordinary endpoint remain available. We may ask for \(0.3000\ldots\text{,}\) or for \(0.2999\ldots\text{.}\) The construction can follow either writing at every ordinary position, possibly in different models. It promises those digits, not an identification of the internal quotient with the external real they name.
    🔗
    Finally choose the halting decimal from Chapter 4. Its digits answer the questions on our list of programs and inputs: does this run stop? No program at our desk prints all those answers. The same fixed \(P,Q\text{,}\) nevertheless, have a suitable model containing a division record that agrees with every one of them in its ordinary positions. The model need not regard that record as its own halting sequence: the answers were chosen outside it. Our two workers are still searching. There has been no surreptitious delivery of the halting answers to their paper.
    🔗
    This certificate reversal adapts the argument Hamkins attributes to Vadim Kosoy. The shared quotient record is the additional fastening that lets our finite requests pass together into compactness. The source notes distinguish this suitable-model conclusion from the stronger extension results associated with universal algorithms.
    🔗
    At the beginning, the worker could follow the directions without understanding what any of it meant. That was their strength: a misplaced borrow could be found without settling a philosophy of number. We have kept that local exactness. Every ordinary subtraction still has its ordinary answer. But when a sentence says that the entire work is finished, the range of objects it speaks about matters. The finite sheet is familiar; the completed computation has brought a world with it. Long division is still waiting at the desk, ready for its next zero.
