    Chapter 4 Instructions about instructions

    Six digits lie on the page. We are waiting for the seventh. The worker has filled three more sheets with scratch work, so there is certainly activity; whether any of it will become a digit is another matter. Our game needs the next digit in order to oppose it. A worker can spend the rest of time preparing to be helpful.
    🔗
    Sometimes the instructions give the trouble away. A jump that returns to itself will never reach the answer row. Other sheets take more reading. We might hope to give a second worker the task of inspecting them: decide whether the work eventually stops, however long it takes. To test that hope, we must let instructions become something a worker can read without obeying. The difference is familiar enough to cause trouble in a classroom.
    🔗
    A teacher writes “Stand up” on the board. If the class is copying sentences, everyone can remain seated. The same two words would have a rather different effect midway through a fire drill. Reading a command does not, by itself, tell us whether to obey it.
    🔗
    Our worker needs no judgement about the occasion. The sheet currently being followed determines what happens to the other writing. Consider these three lines:
    🔗
    0: Write(A,7)
1: Move(A,0,1,0)
2: Halt
    Follow the sheet and a 7 appears, card A moves one square right, and the work stops. Copy the sheet and three lines of instructions appear instead. The word Halt receives exactly as much obedience as “Stand up” in the copying exercise: none. A copying worker can write Halt all afternoon without stopping, provided the sheet being followed says to keep copying.
    🔗
    Our copying directions already know how to move between a source and a destination. With instruction text, the alphabet includes letters, punctuation, and line breaks. A space inside a command cannot signal the end of the text, so we save the place just beyond its last character on a card. The copying stops when its moving card reaches that saved place. Spaces receive the same patient attention as digits.
    🔗
    Obeying a supplied sheet is another possible job. The worker can keep a written record of its squares and place cards, with a number saying which supplied instruction comes next. For the little sheet above, the record changes like this:
    🔗
    Next instruction Position on \(A\) Written marks
    Write(A,7) starting square none
    Move(A,0,1,0) starting square 7 in that square
    Halt one square to the right 7 in the starting square
    The 7 stays in its original square when A moves. A written address has changed; nothing has instructed the worker to move the mark.
    🔗
    Replace the last line with these three lines:
    🔗
    2: If(A,7) Goto 4
3: Write(A,8)
4: Halt
    The test at line 2 looks at the new place on A. That square is blank. It therefore selects line 3, which writes 8 there. The finished paper has 7 followed by 8. Looking at the square just left behind would have sent the work down the wrong branch.
    🔗
    We could follow all of this on a second page by writing down where everything is. The sentence “square \((0,0,0)\) contains 7” might itself sit halfway down page twelve. Nobody needs to drag it back to the square it describes. When the supplied instruction moves card A, we change the recorded address; when it writes a mark, we change the recorded mark. The configuration in this written account must change exactly as the original configuration would.
    🔗
    This is simulation. The simulator finds the next supplied instruction, matches its kind, and updates the account. Give it a new program and it has new writing to work on. Its own directions for doing that work stay the same.
    🔗
    Every configuration reached after finitely many steps occupies a finite record. Its addresses and marks can be found by finite scans. The machine appendix describes those scans; we call the resulting job Run. If the supplied calculation stops, its simulation returns the result. If it keeps working, the simulator keeps following. This is how instructions become something other instructions can use.
    🔗
    For our first experiment with making sheets, MATH will do. Four letters are enough to keep the copying visible. Here is a program that returns them:
    🔗
    Write(OUT,M)
Move(OUT,0,1,0)
Write(OUT,A)
Move(OUT,0,1,0)
Write(OUT,T)
Move(OUT,0,1,0)
Write(OUT,H)
Halt
    Card OUT starts at a fresh result row. The letters to write are already in the instructions. The sheet would return the same word if its incoming data were CAT, 8, or nothing.
    🔗
    Now give a worker the word MATH and a different task: prepare that program. For its first letter, write a command to put M at the output place, then a command to move right. Continue with A, another move, and T, then move and write the final H. No further move is needed; finish with a halt. The result is the sheet above. Given CAT, the same preparation would make a shorter sheet.
    🔗
    Lay the results side by side. One is four letters; the other is a page of commands for producing four letters. The second worker has finished a perfectly real job even if nobody ever follows those commands. We have made something that can act later, while doing nothing more energetic now than moving marks across paper.
    🔗
    Call the preparation Print. Thus Print(MATH) is the text of the program above, not its four-letter output. If we supply a whole instruction sheet as the incoming text, Print prepares a program that will return every character of that sheet. Its copied command Halt is still only part of the returned text. The printer’s own halt ends the work.
    🔗
    We also need to pass a result from one job to another. Put our MATH printer beside a job that copies its input and appends an exclamation mark. Following them in that order gives
    \begin{equation*} \texttt{MATH}\qquad\hbox{and then}\qquad\texttt{MATH!}. \end{equation*}
    The second job would turn CAT into CAT! just as readily. Its input need not have come from this particular printer.
    🔗
    We can assemble one sheet containing both jobs and directions to run the first, then give its returned result to the second:
    🔗
    First supplied text Second supplied text
    The recipe Print(MATH) The recipe that appends !
    The two boxes contain texts. While preparing them, the worker copies instructions without obeying them. Later, running the assembled sheet passes the result across:
    \begin{equation*} \text{run left}\ \longrightarrow\ \texttt{MATH} \ \longrightarrow\ \text{run right}\ \longrightarrow\ \texttt{MATH!}. \end{equation*}
    Call this assembly Combine(p,q), where \(p\) and \(q\) are the two supplied texts. The preparation always finishes: it copies two finite texts into fixed surrounding directions. Running the result is another matter. If the first job never ends, the second waits forever for its input. The worker assembling the sheet need not wait along with it. An endless job can take very little paper to describe.
    🔗
    Fix the assembly down to its spaces and punctuation. Preparing it twice from the same two texts must give identical sheets. The workshop in Appendix C does one assembly character by character. This small insistence on copying exactly will soon let us compare a program with its own result. For now, the jobs are separate: Print prepares a printer, Combine joins jobs, and Run follows the resulting directions.
    🔗
    For Alonzo Church, the manipulation of expressions was itself a subject for mathematics. His student Stephen Kleene followed that idea into self-reference. A program’s description need not remain outside its reach, in the hands of a person watching it run.
    🔗
    Try to write a sheet that returns its own complete instructions. “Copy this sheet” sounds promising, but our worker follows a saved place, not the word “this.” We have supplied no instruction for locating the running sheet. Putting a literal copy inside it looks worse: the copy must include the copying directions, including their copy. There is a great deal of paper available, but this is a poor way to begin using it.
    🔗
    We can leave room on the desk. A preparation that works on any supplied text will let us arrange the copying in three separate stages. Take three sheets of different colours so that we can watch which is which.
    🔗
    Take a white sheet. Its job is to prepare a two-part package: first a printer for the supplied text, then that text itself as a program to be run on the printer’s result. In our notation, its job is to form Combine(Print(input),input). The incoming text must describe a valid program. White returns the package; it does not execute it.
    🔗
    Try white on the exclamation-mark program. White produces a package which, if followed, will first return the exclamation-program text, then append an exclamation mark to that text. There is no special power here: the earlier MATH example is doing the same work on a longer, more interesting string of characters.
    🔗
    White’s own instructions are now finished. We can supply them as input too. Follow white once, using its complete text as the supplied text, and put the resulting package on a blue sheet. Blue contains:
    🔗
    First supplied text Second supplied text
    Print(white text) The white text itself
    We know what is in the left box because white’s first preparation always makes a printer for its input. We know what is in the right box because white’s second preparation always copies that input. Here the input happened to be white.
    🔗
    Now run blue, and reserve a green sheet for its result.
    🔗
    Blue’s first part returns white’s text. Its second part obeys white, with precisely that text as input. What does white do with its own text? We have just performed this job ourselves: it makes the package on blue. The same input and the same assembly therefore put the same package on green.
    🔗
    Put green beside blue. Every character agrees. We followed blue and obtained blue’s complete text on another sheet.
    🔗
    We can stop comparing now, and the worker can stop working. White’s job ends when it has prepared the package. Nobody has instructed it to run the new package, then the next one, filling the room with green paper. Blue returns its own description and finishes.
    🔗
    White and blue have different instructions; blue and green have the same ones. The route from one to the other is short enough to leave on the desk:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rcl} \text{white, given white's text}&\longrightarrow&\text{blue's text},\\[3pt] \text{blue's first part}&\longrightarrow&\text{white's text},\\[3pt] \text{blue's second part, given that text}&\longrightarrow&\text{blue's text}. \end{array} \end{equation*}
    The last result was written on green. Nothing in the work required the worker to recognize a reflection. The copied characters agree because both preparations used precisely the same ingredients.
    🔗
    A self-reproducing program of this kind is called a quine. We can record its construction without the colours. For valid program text \(n\text{,}\) let
    \begin{equation*} D(n)=\texttt{Combine}(\texttt{Print}(n),n). \end{equation*}
    If \(b\) is white’s text, white performs the preparation \(D\text{.}\) Blue’s text is \(D(b)\text{.}\) Running blue supplies \(b\) to white, which returns \(D(b)\text{:}\) the very text of blue. The equation follows the same three sheets; it adds no new machinery.
    🔗
    Our copy need not remain a souvenir. Once the description is on the page, another job can read it.
    🔗
    Suppose we want to count the characters in the running program. Change white so that, after preparing its two-part package, it counts the characters in that package. Because we have changed white, we must make a new blue sheet from the new white text. When new blue runs, new white reconstructs precisely that blue package and counts it. Even the instructions responsible for the count are included in the count. There is no need to keep adjusting a number written into the program each time the program gets longer.
    🔗
    Nothing about counting was essential. We could examine the description, search for a proof mentioning it, or give it to our proposed inspector.
    🔗
    Stephen Kleene (1909–1994)
    🔗
    Kleene’s recursion theorem says that any mechanical task expecting a program description can be given a program that performs the task on its own description. The program may return a result, print forever, or search without ever printing. We do not have to settle that question in order to construct it.
    🔗
    Here is the construction with an arbitrary chosen task \(T\text{.}\) White prepares the familiar package from its input. Instead of returning the package, it gives that package’s description to \(T\) and performs \(T\text{.}\) Written with our named subroutines, white is:
    🔗
    Call Print(INPUT, printer)
Call Combine(printer, INPUT, package)
Call Task(package, RESULT)
Halt
    Each Call visits the named job and, if that job finishes, returns to the next line. INPUT names the supplied text; printer and package name the places for the two preparations; RESULT holds the task’s returned answer. Only the third call performs \(T\text{.}\) The first two merely build the text it will receive.
    🔗
    Now make blue from this white sheet, exactly as before: first supply white’s text, then run white on it. Blue consequently reconstructs blue and gives that description to \(T\text{.}\) Calling the modified white text \(b_T\text{,}\) and the resulting blue text \(r=D(b_T)\text{,}\) we can follow the sequence:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{c} \text{Supply }b_T;\\ \text{use it to reconstruct }D(b_T)=r;\\ \text{carry out }T\text{ on }r. \end{array} \end{equation*}
    We finished white before preparing blue. Blue then rebuilt its own description from the finished white text and handed it to \(T\text{.}\) Each preparation had its materials in front of it; none waited for an unfinished copy to contain itself. The circle belongs to the descriptions. The work reaches it by a finite path.
    🔗
    After the reconstruction, the program behaves as \(T\) does on that description. The preparation costs extra steps and scratch paper. It changes neither the returned result nor the permanent digits, and it finishes before the chosen task begins. If \(T\) never returns, the last halt on white is never reached.
    🔗
    Our inspector can now be asked about a program which will hear the inspector’s answer.
    🔗
    The proposed inspector must accept any valid program, started with no incoming data, and eventually say either stops or continues forever. It must always finish and always be right. Some sheets make its job easy. A three-line write–move–halt sheet stops. Again: Goto Again does not. The promise is to handle every other sheet too, using a fixed finite method.
    🔗
    Give our new task a description to submit for inspection. If the verdict is stops, the task loops forever. If the verdict is continues forever, it stops. The answer square contains Y for a predicted halt and N for a predicted endless run:
    🔗
    Call Inspect(INPUT, answer)
If(answer,Y) Goto Forever
Halt
Forever: Goto Forever
    This is only a contrary response to an answer. On its own it proves nothing: a program can behave differently from some other program. Kleene supplies the missing connection. Construct a program that performs this very task on its own complete description. The description submitted for inspection is now the description of the program which will react to the answer.
    🔗
    The inspector’s answer What the complete program then does
    It stops. Begins the endless loop.
    It continues forever. Stops immediately.
    The inspector has run out of answers. Saying that the program stops starts its endless loop. Saying that it continues brings it to a halt. The program need not be clever about either response; its four-line task is positively unimaginative. Kleene’s construction arranged the part that matters: the verdict concerns the very program receiving it.
    🔗
    If the inspector’s promised instructions existed, we could copy them into this construction. The result would be one more valid finite program, hence one more case the inspector had promised to settle. Either answer would fail. The promised inspector cannot exist.
    🔗
    This is the halting problem: no mechanical method always finishes and correctly decides whether any supplied program will stop. Particular stopping questions can still be answered, as our two easy examples show. An inspector that admits uncertainty or sometimes waits forever may be useful, but it has surrendered the universal promise we tested.
    🔗
    A program can also be given a particular input. That does not evade the argument. A literal printer first supplies the input, and the original program follows it; the combined sheet starts with no incoming data. It carries the chosen input inside its text.
    🔗
    One modest inspector remains available. Run the program; if it halts, announce the halt. Every actual halt eventually gets its announcement. A program that never halts gives this inspector no occasion to announce a negative answer.
    🔗
    The missing digit cannot have a universal inspector either. Given any program, simulate it on private paper and write a 7 only when that simulation halts. An inspector that always decided whether this sheet would ever write its digit would decide whether the supplied program halts. The unanswered question has followed us back to the decimal row.
    🔗
    That silence can be written into a decimal.
    🔗
    Give a new worker any program to simulate on private scratch pages. Before each simulated working step, append a zero to an answer row beginning \(0.\text{.}\) If the simulated program reaches its halt, write a single 1 in the next answer place, and then zeros forever. The simulated marks stay on the scratch pages; only this worker’s chosen zeros and one reach the decimal row.
    🔗
    Here a working step means a step before the halt instruction is reached. If the supplied program takes two such steps before halting, the answer is
    \begin{equation*} 0.0010000\ldots. \end{equation*}
    If it takes five, the answer is
    \begin{equation*} 0.0000010000\ldots. \end{equation*}
    If it never halts, the answer is zero. In every case each next digit appears after finitely much work. The worker keeps writing even when the private simulation has no end.
    🔗
    Put this sheet beside a sheet that simply writes zeros forever. Do they name the same number? They do exactly when the simulated program never stops. An isolated 1, wherever it appears, makes a positive number. A universal method for deciding equality of these decimal printers would therefore settle the halting problem.
    🔗
    Suppose both rows still show only zeros. Put a finger under the first pair, then the next. Every comparison succeeds. The next zero will be another successful comparison, and we would like it to do rather more: guarantee all the comparisons still to come. A zero has no such authority. Both workers keep supplying digits after finite work; the question that cannot always be settled is whether their completed decimals agree. We have moved the uncertainty off the answer row and onto the scratch pages.
    🔗
    Replacing that single 1 by the growing-gap printer makes another version: zero if the simulation never stops, an irrational decimal if it does. The workshop follows both that variation and the question of recognizing endless printers. Their common obstacle is already here, hidden in a row of apparently innocent zeros.
    🔗
    There is a more concentrated way to put the difficulty on one row. List every valid program together with every possible finite input. The descriptions are finite texts, so our earlier ordering by length and alphabetical order will reach every pair. Checking the grammar never asks whether the program will eventually stop.
    🔗
    Assign the first place the digit 1 if the first pair halts, and 0 if it does not. Give the second place the answer to the second question, and so on. These are mathematical assignments of values, not directions our worker has learned to carry out. They define a decimal \(H\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} H=0.h_1h_2h_3\ldots, \end{equation*}
    Each place now has a definite question attached to it. That is enough to define the decimal; it has not provided a worker who can print it. Suppose a finite sheet did print all the answers. To settle the twentieth halting question, we would wait for digit twenty. For the millionth, we would wait much longer. But every particular wait would finish, since this sheet was supposed to print every digit. Its steady row of zeros and ones would deliver every verdict our impossible inspector was asked to deliver.
    🔗
    Nor can another decimal name make this real computable. The decimal contains no nines and contains infinitely many ones: an immediately halting program can be paired with infinitely many inputs. It is neither eventually zero nor eventually nine. Our earlier comparison of decimal names therefore leaves it only this one expansion.
    🔗
    Baire found room outside every proposed list of computable reals. We now have a particular real in that room, made entirely from questions about the finite sheets on our own desk. The ingredients are familiar; the completed collection of answers is beyond every sheet that could print it.
    🔗
    We might still hope to settle one question at a time. Mathematicians do that with proofs. A worker could search for a proof about its next digit and write the digit when the proof arrives. After all, we have just taught instructions to read instructions. A proof is another piece of writing.
    🔗
    The worker who receives it, however, may have instructions of its own about what to do next.
