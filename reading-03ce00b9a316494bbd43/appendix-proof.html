    Appendix B. A proof with the work left in

    Two bags have something in common. We have not opened them, and nobody has told us what the shared object is. Even so, neither bag can be empty. That is the whole argument we will spread across the next few pages. Its conclusion is modest enough to let us watch what happens to every assumption along the way.
    🔗
    Call the bags \(b\) and \(c\text{.}\) As in Chapter 5, \(n\in b\) says that the object called \(n\) is a member of \(b\text{.}\) The letter need not stand for a number. We could be talking about another bag. The sign \(\land\) joins two statements with “and,” so
    \begin{equation*} (n\in b)\land(n\in c) \end{equation*}
    says that this same object belongs to both bags. Keeping the name the same matters. A red marble in one bag and a blue marble in the other would not establish that they have a member in common.
    🔗
    At present we do not have the object, or even a name for it. We have only the promise that one exists. Put that promise on paper:
    \begin{equation*} \exists x\,\bigl((x\in b)\land(x\in c)\bigr). \end{equation*}
    Read \(\exists x\) as “there is an object \(x\) such that.” The rest specifies what it must do: belong to \(b\) and to \(c\text{.}\) Here \(x\) is a place in the sentence, waiting for an example. The sentence does not tell us which example fills it.
    🔗
    We can nevertheless give the unknown object a temporary name. Let \(n\) be that name, unused elsewhere in the argument. Open a branch of the proof and write
    \begin{equation*} \bigl[(n\in b)\land(n\in c)\bigr]_1. \end{equation*}
    The brackets mark a temporary assumption. The small 1 is its label, so that we can later say exactly which assumption has finished its job. We are investigating what follows from the promised example, whichever object it is. We have not reached into a bag and selected our favourite marble.
    🔗
    From an “and” statement we may take either part. This is one of the proof cards from Chapter 5, now doing an actual job:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{(n\in b)\land(n\in c)}{n\in c}. \end{equation*}
    The statement above the bar is available; the statement below it is the part we keep. A worker checking the step compares the writing on the two sides of the bar. If the lower line said \(c\in n\text{,}\) the check would fail. Rearranging the furniture inside a formula can change who contains whom.
    🔗
    We could also take \(n\in b\text{,}\) then join the two pieces again. Combining information does no more, and no less, than preserve its parts:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{n\in b\qquad n\in c} {(n\in b)\land(n\in c)}. \end{equation*}
    Each part still depends on assumption 1. Putting them on one line has not made that assumption disappear. A checker keeps track of these dependencies along with the statements themselves.
    🔗
    The shorter route to our conclusion is already open. Since \(n\in c\text{,}\) there is a member of \(c\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{n\in c}{\exists y(y\in c)}. \end{equation*}
    This time we are giving an example for an existence claim. The name \(n\) appears above the bar, but the conclusion below does not mention it. We can close branch 1 and keep that conclusion. We began with a promise of a shared member and have derived a promise of a member of \(c\text{,}\) without finding out what either promise names. Taking the first part instead gives the same argument for \(b\text{.}\) Neither bag can be empty.
    🔗
    There are two permissions here, and they run in opposite directions. A known example lets us assert that an example exists. A bare existence claim lets us investigate an unknown example under a temporary name. The second permission does not deliver an object to the desk. It allows an argument to proceed with the information already supplied.
    🔗
    Try carrying \(n\in c\) out of the branch instead. Now the conclusion still uses \(n\text{.}\) Outside this branch, what does that name mean? We never assigned it a particular object there. The checker refuses the move. By contrast, “\(c\) has a member” makes no demand on the borrowed name. We can take that claim with us when the branch closes.
    🔗
    The name must be new when the branch opens, too. Suppose a marble already called \(n\) is known to be outside \(c\text{.}\) If we used its name for the promised common member, we would obtain both “\(n\) belongs to \(c\)” and “\(n\) does not belong to \(c\text{.}\)” The shared member might have been a button all along. Our choice of a letter would have created the trouble. The worker can prevent this by inspecting the names in the starting claim, in the conclusion we intend to keep, and in the other assumptions still in use. The temporary name must be absent from all three.
    🔗
    Now take the longer route, through a contradiction. In addition to the shared-member promise, suppose \(e\) names an empty bag. We want to show that \(c\) is not \(e\text{.}\) Begin branch 1 afresh, again naming the unknown common member \(n\text{.}\) Open another branch inside it: try \(c=e\text{.}\) Equality lets us replace one name by the other. From \(n\in c\) we therefore obtain \(n\in e\text{.}\)
    🔗
    But \(e\) was empty. In particular, \(n\) is not in it. Write this as \(\neg(n\in e)\text{,}\) using \(\neg\) for “not.” We have reached a statement and its negation. The contradiction rejects the equality we tried. All the work can remain on the sheet:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ll} \bigl[(n\in b)\land(n\in c)\bigr]_1 &\text{name the promised member}\\[3pt] \quad n\in c&\text{take the second part}\\[3pt] \quad[c=e]_2&\text{try equality}\\[3pt] \qquad n\in e&\text{replace equal names}\\[3pt] \qquad\neg(n\in e)&\text{use emptiness}\\[3pt] \qquad\bot&\text{contradiction}\\[3pt] \quad\neg(c=e)&\text{close assumption 2}\\[3pt] \neg(c=e)&\text{close branch 1}. \end{array} \end{equation*}
    The symbol \(\bot\) marks the contradiction. Indentation shows which trials are still open. The worker follows the labels and the dependencies of the lines; the indentation helps our eyes.
    🔗
    The two final lines say the same thing. They do different work. The first closes the attempted equality while the unknown member still has its temporary name. The second closes the branch in which that name was introduced. Our conclusion mentions only \(c\) and \(e\text{,}\) so the temporary \(n\) can go. What remains is exactly the claim we wanted, resting on the shared-member promise and the emptiness of \(e\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The line \(n\in e\) is still visible. We cannot reuse it as an established fact. It depended on the equality we rejected. Nor have we disproved the starting promise: an empty \(e\) can coexist quite peacefully with two other bags sharing a button. The contradiction tells us to reject the particular trial that produced it while the earlier assumptions remain in force. Naming that trial is part of the proof.
    🔗
    This is why a proof contains more information than a list of sentences. The lines must say where their authority comes from. An arithmetic worksheet keeps the borrowed one beside the column that needs it. Our proof sheet keeps a borrowed assumption beside the lines that need it. Moving either across the page without keeping its connection can manufacture a very persuasive wrong answer.
    🔗
    An unknown example is also different from an arbitrary object. Our \(n\) was unknown, but it was not unrestricted: the starting promise required it to belong to both bags. We cannot declare that every object belongs to \(c\) just because \(n\) does. We have followed one member under a temporary name, not investigated everything in the world.
    🔗
    For a genuinely arbitrary object, recall the two permissions of Adjunctive Set Theory. There is an empty collection, and we may add one object to a collection already available. Start with any object \(a\text{.}\) Take an empty collection and add \(a\text{,}\) obtaining a collection \(d\text{.}\) Then \(a\in d\text{,}\) so we can give \(d\) as an example for “there is a collection containing \(a\text{,}\)” written \(\exists y(a\in y)\text{.}\) This claim no longer needs \(d\) or the name of the empty collection. Close the branches in which those names were introduced and keep the claim. We used nothing special about \(a\text{:}\) it did not have to be a marble, an empty bag, or a member already promised to us. The argument therefore applies to every object. In symbols its conclusion is
    \begin{equation*} \forall a\,\exists d\,(a\in d). \end{equation*}
    The sign \(\forall a\) means “for every object \(a\text{.}\)” Read the signs in their written order: every object has some collection containing it. We have not made one enormous collection containing everything. A different \(a\) may require a different \(d\text{.}\)
    🔗
    These little arguments are useful because we can see the errors as well as the successful moves. Reversing the names in membership changes the statement. Exporting the unknown member keeps a name we were allowed to borrow only temporarily. Generalizing from that member mistakes a restriction for freedom. None of these errors requires the checker to be offended by the conclusion. Each leaves a defect in the written argument.
    🔗
    Return finally to the worker who writes 7, moves one square right, and halts. A record of the work can show the blank starting square, the appearance of the 7, the change of the worker’s place, and the halt. It must leave the written 7 where it was. Moving the worker does not give the digit permission to follow. Chapter 5 explains how the coding results let us express these checks in our set language and turn an actual completed run into a formal proof of its completion.
    🔗
    The existence claim then concerns a record rather than a member of a pictured bag. The logical moves are the ones we have just used. If a proof begins by assuming such a record exists, it may name it temporarily and reason about it. That does not execute the program. A claim that the worker has finished and a worker who has finished remain different things; the chapters about Henkin’s models depend on keeping the difference visible.
    🔗
    The complete rule specifications and the coding results supporting that application are collected in the separate technical companion. Here the whole proof fits on a sheet. We can point to the place where the unknown member enters, to the equality that fails, and to the line that is allowed to survive it.
