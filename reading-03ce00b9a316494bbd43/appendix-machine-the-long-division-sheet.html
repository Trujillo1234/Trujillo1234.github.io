    Section A.6 The long-division sheet

    The division \(123/8\) begins with remainder zero. Bring down one: no eight fits. Bring down two beside that one, making twelve: one subtraction leaves four, and a second fails. Write one in the quotient. Bring down three beside four, making forty-three: five subtractions leave three, so write five. The numerator has ended. Write the point and begin bringing down zeros.
    🔗
    These few sentences conceal several places to remember. N follows the numerator, while NU saves its last digit. DL and DU save the divisor’s first and last digits. Q marks the next quotient square. For the accepted remainder, RL and RU hold the leftmost and units places. The next unused trial page has its units square saved on FRESH.
    🔗
    One concrete arrangement puts the numerator on page zero, row zero, and the divisor on page zero, row two, each starting at column zero. The quotient begins at column zero, row zero of page one. The initial remainder zero goes at \((2,0,0)\text{,}\) named by RU; the first trial uses \((3,0,0)\text{,}\) named by FRESH. Each later trial takes the next page. FindUnits supplies NU and DU from the supplied first places.
    🔗
    Six separate reminder squares occupy column zero, rows two through seven, of page one. They are COUNT, C, STATUS, PHASE, PRINTED, and DONE. The first counts successful trials; the next two belong to subtraction. The remaining three answer questions that affect the writing of the quotient. Has the decimal point appeared? Has the integer answer begun? Has the last numerator digit come down? Each starts blank and becomes one when its answer becomes yes.
    🔗
    Two short tasks finish the preparations. Copy(A,B) looks at the digit or blank at A and writes that same mark at B, leaving both addresses fixed. Eleven tests cover its eleven possible observations; the seven test writes seven, the blank test erases, and each then jumps past the remaining tests. This differs from Place, which copies an address. Increment(COUNT) increases a digit by one: test zero and write one, test one and write two, and continue up to the last case, which tests eight and writes nine. Each of these nine choices then resumes the next instruction. Any other mark halts. A division round will never ask this little sheet to increase nine.
    🔗
    At trial below, subtraction gets the accepted remainder, the divisor, and a fresh answer page. Success moves the remainder cards to the new difference and increases the count. Failure jumps straight to settled, leaving those cards where they were. The sheet begins by checking the divisor. A moving card, CHECK, copies its first place from DL and walks through its digits looking for a nonzero one. If every digit is zero, the sheet writes Z at STATUS and halts.

    Follow the first round after the point, starting with remainder three. bringzero writes zero to its right, making thirty. prepare tidies the remainder if needed and resets the count. The trials now make \(30-8=22\text{,}\) \(22-8=14\text{,}\) and \(14-8=6\text{.}\) Each success gives the remainder a new address. \(6-8\) fails on yet another page. The accepted six and count three survive, so fractionalanswer copies three into the waiting quotient square. The next zero is appended to six, making sixty. Seven successes leave four; the following round gives five successes and zero. The display has reached \(15.375\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Before the point, PRINTED lets the initial unsuccessful division of one by eight pass without writing an unwanted leading zero. If the whole integer answer is zero, as in \(1/4\text{,}\) point writes that one necessary zero before the point. Afterward, zeros have their full place value and every settled digit is written. The sheet therefore continues with \(15.375000\ldots\text{,}\) or \(0.250000\ldots\) for \(1/4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Why can one counting square suffice? With divisor eight, the remainder before a new digit comes down is at most seven. Even bringing down nine makes only seventy-nine, less than ten eights. In general, a remainder smaller than a positive whole-number divisor is at least one smaller. Multiplying it by ten leaves room for ten more; adding at most nine still falls short of ten divisors. At most nine trials succeed, and the next fails. Each trial crosses finitely many columns, so each round eventually writes its digit.
