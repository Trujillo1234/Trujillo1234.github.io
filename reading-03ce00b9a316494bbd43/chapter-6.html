    Chapter 6 A world for the words

    Writing 314 takes three strokes of the pen, give or take one’s handwriting. Supplying the computation that puts it there is another matter. Our searcher \(E\) has been told to find a proof forbidding an output and then produce that output. Consistency keeps every such proof out of its reach. The search continues; the answer row stays empty. Yet the sentence “\(E\) finishes with 314” can be added to our assumptions without producing a contradiction.
    🔗
    We cannot settle this by writing the answer for it. A checker would want the preceding configuration, and the one before that, all the way back to the blank starting page. A declaration of success has incurred a debt of steps. Each must be accounted for, under the very instructions that keep our worker searching.
    🔗
    Leon Henkin found a way to start with consistent declarations and build a setting that fulfills them. He did not begin by finding the objects his sentences mentioned. He began by giving them names. Henkin was another of Church’s doctoral students; his doctorate came in 1947, and his completeness proof appeared in 1949. Gödel had already proved completeness. Henkin’s construction gave the result a particularly tangible form: the writing itself could supply the materials of a world. In his later account he credited Church’s teaching and writing with the main ideas behind his discovery.
    🔗
    That is a considerable assignment for some ink. Before asking it for the missing computation, let us see what it can do with three letters. Suppose a consistent notebook permits these entries:
    \begin{equation*} a\in b,\qquad a\ne b. \end{equation*}
    Something named \(a\) belongs to something named \(b\text{,}\) and they are different objects. An empty set and the set containing it would meet these two demands. We need not choose those particular objects; for the moment the notebook records only what any eventual choice must respect.
    🔗
    The first entry also says that \(b\) has a member:
    \begin{equation*} \exists x\,(x\in b). \end{equation*}
    Give that promised member a new name, \(c\text{,}\) and add \(c\in b\text{.}\) Then ask whether \(c=a\text{.}\) In this example we may answer yes. The new name can name the member we already had. We have used another letter without multiplying the population.
    🔗
    Here is a little world that the entries are beginning to describe:
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{\{a,c\}}_{\text{names of one object}} \quad\xrightarrow{\quad\text{belongs to}\quad}\quad \underbrace{\{b\}}_{\text{names of another object}}. \end{equation*}
    Call the left object \(U\text{,}\) the right one \(V\text{.}\) The arrow gives \(U\) a place in \(V\text{.}\) It answers the claim \(a\in b\) and the claim \(c\in b\) at once, and supplies the member promised by “there is.” The words have begun to acquire things they are true of.
    🔗
    They have not yet supplied a whole set-theoretic world. We have only a fragment, and even that fragment leaves questions open:
    🔗
    belongs to \(U\text{,}\) named by \(a,c\) \(V\text{,}\) named by \(b\)
    \(U\text{,}\) named by \(a,c\) undecided yes
    \(V\text{,}\) named by \(b\) undecided undecided
    An undecided square is not a no. More writing must settle it. And there will be many more names: the axioms demand further sets, whose members in turn need places in the picture. What makes Henkin’s construction work is a way of continuing this small exercise without losing consistency or leaving an existence promise unfulfilled.
    🔗
    The new name matters. Before we introduced \(c\text{,}\) it had no commitments in the notebook. An old name might already have been ruled out as a member of the set in question; forcing that name to serve as the promised member would manufacture a contradiction. A fresh name avoids this trouble. It need not denote a new object, as \(c=a\) just showed. It needs to arrive without a past in the notebook.
    🔗
    We can prepare for an existence claim even before deciding whether it is true. Our notebook already uses \(c\text{,}\) so reserve an unused name, \(e\text{,}\) for the next promise. For a condition \(A(x)\text{,}\) enter
    \begin{equation*} (\exists x\,A(x))\longrightarrow A(e). \end{equation*}
    We could take \(A(x)\) to be \(x\in a\text{,}\) asking this time whether \(a\) has a member. The entry says: if anything meets the condition, \(e\) will name an example. If nothing does, it demands nothing of \(e\text{.}\) This is a permanent naming arrangement. The temporary witness in a proof branch disappeared when the branch closed; this name stays available for later conversation.
    🔗
    Why is that arrangement safe? Suppose the new promise about membership in \(a\) produced a contradiction. Close the assumption containing the promise, using our proof-by-contradiction rule. The old notebook would then prove that the promise was false. An “if” fails only when its first part holds and its second part fails, so the old notebook would prove both
    \begin{equation*} \exists x\,(x\in a) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \neg(e\in a). \end{equation*}
    But \(e\) is absent from every remaining assumption. Nothing in that proof of nonmembership could depend on which object \(e\) named. Our arbitrary-example rule therefore gives \(\forall x\,\neg(x\in a)\text{:}\) nobody belongs to \(a\text{.}\) The existence claim lets us temporarily call one member \(d\text{.}\) We obtain \(d\in a\) and \(\neg(d\in a)\text{,}\) and close the temporary branch with their contradiction. The old notebook could already contradict itself. If it was consistent, the supposed trouble from our new promise cannot occur.
    🔗
    Nothing in this reasoning depended on membership being the condition. Replace \(x\in a\) by \(A(x)\) and the same proof works. The freshness restriction from our proof cards has earned its keep: once the trial assumption is gone, the remaining proof treats \(e\) as an arbitrary object.
    🔗
    We must also finish the undecided squares, and eventually every other question the language can ask. Does \(V\) belong to \(U\text{?}\) Try the two possible additions, \(b\in a\) and \(\neg(b\in a)\text{.}\) At least one preserves consistency. If the first caused a contradiction, the rejection rule would give its negation from the old notebook. If the second also caused a contradiction, classical reductio would give the positive claim. The two failures would already be a contradiction in the old notebook. The same argument applies to any sentence, however elaborate its writing.
    🔗
    This gives us somewhere to go. It does not give the worker a reliable way to tell which way. Looking for a contradiction can reveal one; failing to find it so far is still only failing to find it so far. The construction may require choices no instruction sheet can make. We encountered this gap in the decimal game: proving that room exists for a move is different from programming every move.
    🔗
    Call our starting collection of assumptions \(T\text{.}\) Keep a numbered reserve of new names, none appearing in \(T\text{,}\) and arrange two lists of jobs: decide a sentence; provide a fresh-name promise for an existence statement. Alternate between the lists. All finite expressions in the enlarged language can be listed, so we include sentences involving the new names as well as the old ones. At a decision, take a consistent choice. At a promise, choose a name unused in the work so far and absent from the formula being considered. Even if \(T\) is infinite, it uses none of the reserve names, and only finitely many have been used at any finite stage. We cannot run out of names before the next job.
    🔗
    Every stage is consistent. Could the whole endless notebook somehow become contradictory? A proof of contradiction would be a finite tree. Circle the entries it uses. There are only finitely many, so all were already present at some finite stage. We would have had a contradiction there. An infinite supply of pages does not give a finite proof an infinite supply of premises.
    🔗
    Include all the notebook’s provable consequences and call the result \(K\text{.}\) Each sentence is decided, and each existence claim has a name ready for an example. We have reached this object by an existence argument, not by finishing the worker’s to-do list. Now we can extend the construction of \(U\) and \(V\) to all its names.
    🔗
    Put together every pair of names that \(K\) declares equal. Equality rules keep the grouping in order: a name equals itself; an equality can be reversed; and if \(a=c\) and \(c=e\text{,}\) then \(a=e\text{.}\) The groups are called equivalence classes. Make one object for each group. All the names in it will refer to that object. Our earlier \(a\) and \(c\) must stay together, while \(a\ne b\) keeps \(b\) apart from them.
    🔗
    For each pair of groups, use the notebook to fill its square in the membership table. An entry \(a\in b\) puts yes in the square; \(\neg(a\in b)\) puts no. Because \(K\) decides the sentence consistently, exactly one answer is available. Changing from \(a\) to an equal name \(c\) changes nothing: equality substitution requires the same answer for \(c\in b\text{.}\) The relation belongs to the objects, regardless of which of their names we use.
    🔗
    There are two different jobs for membership here. The letter \(a\) is one of the names collected into \(U\text{.}\) The table says whether \(U\) belongs to \(V\) in our constructed world. Looking inside the braces around \(\{b\}\) will not answer that second question. Those braces help us make the objects; the table supplies the relation the notebook calls membership.
    🔗
    A model is such a setting of objects and relations in which all our starting sentences hold together. We have made its objects from names and its membership from the entries in \(K\text{.}\) The remaining question is whether whole sentences, with their “and,” “not,” and “there is,” agree with the world as well. A table of membership facts would be rather a small reward for all that naming.
    🔗
    Take \((a\in b)\wedge(c\in b)\text{.}\) The notebook contains it exactly when it contains both parts: the conjunction rules let us assemble it or take it apart. Our table makes it true exactly when both squares say yes. Thus the notebook and the model give the same answer to the joined claim. For a negation, \(K\) contains exactly one of \(A\) and \(\neg A\text{.}\) Once \(A\) agrees with the model, its negation agrees too: it is in the notebook just when \(A\) is false there.
    🔗
    Existence was the reason for our fresh names. If \(K\) contains \(\exists x\,(x\in b)\text{,}\) the promise gives \(c\in b\text{.}\) The group named by \(c\) is an actual example in the table. For a general condition \(A(x)\text{,}\) its reserved witness name supplies an example in just the same way. The argument also runs back toward the notebook. Suppose an object in the model meets \(A\text{.}\) Every object has a name; choose one of its names, \(t\text{.}\) Once the smaller statement \(A(t)\) agrees with the model, it must be in \(K\text{.}\) The proof rule for giving an example puts \(\exists x\,A(x)\) in \(K\) as well.
    🔗
    There is no circular appeal to the truth of the whole sentence here. Its construction is finite. Start with the equality and membership claims, already checked, then work outward through each “and,” “not,” and “there is.” A layer agrees because its smaller pieces agree. This is the truth lemma: a sentence is in \(K\) exactly when it is true in the constructed world. Our abbreviations for “or,” “if,” and “every” use the same three operations, so they come along too.
    🔗
    The quantifiers range over the objects we made. In \(\exists x(x\in b)\text{,}\) we ask whether one of those objects is a member of the object named by \(b\text{.}\) “Every” asks about every object in that collection. This is the first-order language we have been using. A set may itself be one of its objects, but the quantifiers do not thereby reach every collection that someone outside the model can imagine.
    🔗
    The names were on a numbered list. Replacing each name by its group lists the model’s objects, perhaps several times each. From outside, then, this is a countable model. Its own lists, if its axioms describe lists, are among its objects; our outside numbering need not be one of them. This is the distinction behind Skolem’s observation, which we follow further in Chapter 4 of the Technical Companion.
    🔗
    Every assumption in \(T\) belongs to \(K\text{,}\) so the truth lemma makes all of them true in our model. We have reached Henkin’s model-existence theorem:
    🔗
    Every consistent first-order collection of assumptions in a countable language has a model.
    🔗
    🔗
    Completeness in this setting connects proof with interpretation. It is a different use of the word from the completeness of the real line that kept Baire’s nested intervals from losing their point.
    🔗
    Return now to the debt of steps behind 314. A useful consequence of Henkin’s theorem comes from the same finite proof tree we used to protect the notebook. Suppose every finite selection from a countable collection of requests can be met in some model. If the whole collection proved a contradiction, that proof would use only a finite selection. In the model meeting those requests, every assumption of the proof would be true. Valid proof steps preserve truth; a contradiction cannot be their result. Thus the full collection is consistent, and Henkin supplies a model for it. This is the compactness theorem. The models for the finite selections can differ. What matters is that no finite proof can collect enough premises to cause trouble.
    🔗
    Try a request that becomes harder as we keep making it. Let \(b\) name a natural number, and require
    \begin{equation*} b>0,\qquad b>1,\qquad b>2,\qquad b>3,\qquad\ldots. \end{equation*}
    For this example include the true first-order statements of ordinary natural-number arithmetic, with its order, addition, and multiplication. Any finite selection of the new demands has a largest numeral on the right. Choose \(b\) larger than that numeral and use the ordinary natural numbers as the model. All the selected demands hold. Compactness therefore gives a model in which the entire list holds for one \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Where could that number have come from? We have not asked for \(b\) to exceed every number in the model. That would include \(b\) itself, demanding \(b>b\text{.}\) We asked separately that it exceed zero, one, two, and every other ordinary numeral. The model has objects named by those numerals and also objects beyond them. Its \(b\) is one of the latter, a nonstandard number. Its arithmetic still supplies \(b+1\text{,}\) which is larger.
    🔗
    Nor is there a first nonstandard number just after the ordinary ones. Every positive number has a predecessor under the arithmetic laws we included. Given any ordinary numeral \(n\text{,}\) the list requires \(b>n+1\text{,}\) and hence \(b-1>n\text{.}\) So \(b-1\) also exceeds every ordinary numeral. The same goes for \(b-2\) and \(b-3\text{.}\) There is no last ordinary number to stand on one side of a border, and no first extra number to stand on the other.
    🔗
    The notation can make this look deceptively accessible. Write
    \begin{equation*} 0,1,2,3,\ldots \qquad\qquad \ldots,b-2,b-1,b,b+1,\ldots. \end{equation*}
    Our eyes cross the gap in a moment. They have crossed some blank paper. The display offers two small views of the order; it has given us no ordinary sequence of counting steps that travels from one view to the other. Every ordinary count we finish is still followed by another ordinary count.
    🔗
    A record that a model calls finite can have a length like \(b\text{.}\) It has a last line according to the model, although its length exceeds every ordinary numeral. Calling that line the last does not get our worker there. This is how the missing computation can be supplied without an ordinary search ever finishing.
    🔗
    Chapter 5 proved that our assumptions together with “\(E\) outputs 314” are consistent. Henkin therefore gives a model in which the output sentence holds. The sentence asks for a completed computation record, and the model has one. It must still obey our fixed interpretation of instructions and records. Those laws prescribe the decoded starting configuration and the unique decoded successor at each nonhalting instruction: the specified mark changes, or the specified place card moves, while everything else stays put.
    🔗
    These requirements are stronger than the earlier promise that an actual successful calculation can be proved successful. They also control what the formulas say across models. Chapter 2 of the Technical Companion sets out the required arithmetic and record laws. Apply them once: the model’s next configuration agrees with the actual worker’s. Apply them again: so does the next. Repeat the argument for any ordinary finite number of steps. Every one agrees, and none is a halt. The completed record in the model must end beyond every ordinary stage.
    🔗
    The model cannot merely announce 314 at that remote last line. The search has one way out, so its record includes what it accepts as an exclusion certificate, the check of that certificate, and the return of the forbidden digits. An ordinary finite certificate would be disastrous: our worker could reach it, respond with the digits, and supply the opposite proof. That is exactly the contradiction consistency ruled out. The model’s certificate cannot be brought back as an ordinary finite proof for us to check.
    🔗
    The objects over which “there is a finite record” ranges have changed. The instruction at an ordinary step has not. We can now let two searches stand beside the old division sheet, one to supply its numerator and one its denominator. Their results and the division will have to stay together in a single record. Then each new digit we ask for can place another demand on that same calculation. The notebook is ready; the division has some requests to make.
