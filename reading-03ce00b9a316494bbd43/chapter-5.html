    Chapter 5 Proof on paper

    The answer row is blank. Give the program a proof that it cannot return 314, and its instructions say to copy those three digits onto the row and stop. The proof would be the occasion for the very event it rules out.
    🔗
    Three digits fit on a scrap of paper. Kleene has arranged the delicate part: the description mentioned by the proof can belong to this very program, including its copying instructions. What we still owe the worker is a way to recognize a proof. A sternly worded note will not do, however plainly we write “cannot.”
    🔗
    Put a long division beside an explanation of why its answer is right. In the calculation, a checker can point to a bad subtraction. In the explanation, a word like “clearly” may be doing the work of three missing lines. Sometimes the reader supplies them without noticing. Sometimes “clearly” is where the afternoon goes. Our worker cannot make that contribution: the missing steps must be written down.
    🔗
    David Hilbert made this demand central to his study of mathematics’ foundations. A formal proof would be a finite written object whose steps obey exact rules. Finding it might take imagination; checking it would take the rules and the page. The checker need not appreciate the theorem or know what the symbols describe. This is a remarkable kind of trust to place in writing. The writer’s insight can disappear from view while the proof remains available to another pair of eyes.
    🔗
    It can also become available to our waiting program. A proof that seemed to be the final word about its behaviour would become something the program reads before deciding what to write. We will build the proof rules carefully enough to find out whether that proof can arrive.
    🔗
    We have a tiny completed calculation already: write 7, move one square right, and halt. Its trace records what happens at each instruction boundary:
    🔗
    Entry Next instruction Card \(A\) Marked squares
    0 Write(A,7) \((0,0,0)\) none
    1 Move(A,0,1,0) \((0,0,0)\) 7 at \((0,0,0)\)
    2 Halt \((0,1,0)\) 7 at \((0,0,0)\)
    Call this record \(r\text{.}\) Its first entry gives the prescribed beginning. Each following entry must carry out the instruction in the previous one. The final entry reaches the halt. A move changes the place card while preserving every marked square; a record that moves the 7 along with the card fails the check.
    🔗
    For this little program, “it halts” comes with something we can put on the desk: the completed record. The same words about our searcher ask for a record we cannot supply in advance. We do not know how long it would be. A sentence must be able to describe the required record without having the record sitting beside it.
    🔗
    That is where words such as “and,” “not,” and “there is” earn their place. We need the first entry to be right and the next entry to follow correctly, and all the later entries to do likewise. We need to say that there is an object meeting the whole description. Our arguments already use these words. Against the growing-gap decimal we tried a repeating block, separated two cases, and found a contradiction in each. With long division we took an arbitrary denominator and followed its remainders. The difficulty for a checker is knowing what may be kept after an argument has tried something and found it impossible. Pencil marks do not withdraw themselves when a supposition fails.
    🔗
    Sets will hold the pieces of the records. A bag is a useful picture to begin with: it has members, possibly other bags, but no built-in left-to-right order. Names let us refer to the objects. If the object named \(a\) is a member of the object named \(b\text{,}\) write
    \begin{equation*} a\in b. \end{equation*}
    The sign \(\in\) means “is a member of.” Suppose we also have \(b\in c\text{.}\) We can keep both facts in one statement:
    \begin{equation*} (a\in b)\land(b\in c). \end{equation*}
    The new sign \(\land\) means “and.” Here is the move as a proof tree:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a\in b\qquad b\in c} {(a\in b)\land(b\in c)}\quad\text{keep both}. \end{equation*}
    Read the tree downward. The facts above the bar allow the statement below it. We can also take either part of a conjunction out and use it separately. To check this particular move, compare the strings: both premises must survive intact in the conclusion. The bags can remain closed.
    🔗
    They can even mislead us if we lean too heavily on the picture. From \(a\in b\) and \(b\in c\text{,}\) it does not follow that \(a\in c\text{.}\) An object in an inner bag need not be a direct member of the outer one. We are good at picturing nested bags and a little too good at forgetting which bag we opened. The printed rule asks for the exact two statements, which prevents that shortcut.
    🔗
    On a general card, let capital letters stand for whole statements:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{A\qquad B}{A\land B}. \end{equation*}
    The same card can join two short membership claims or two elaborate claims about a computation. In our trace, “entry zero is the right beginning” and “entry one follows correctly” will be statements joined in just this way. The card’s size stays fixed as the sentences grow.
    🔗
    Suppose \(e\) has no members, and \(e\) belongs to \(b\text{.}\) Could \(b\) be \(e\) itself?
    🔗
    Try it. If \(b=e\text{,}\) replacing \(b\) by \(e\) in \(e\in b\) gives \(e\in e\text{.}\) But \(e\) has no members. The trial has made us assert a statement and its opposite, so the equality must be rejected.
    🔗
    We write “not” as \(\neg\text{,}\) and mark the temporary assumption with brackets and a label:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{l} \text{Already available: }e\in b\text{ and }\neg(e\in e).\\[3pt] [b=e]_{4}\quad\text{try this assumption}\phantom{.}\\ \quad e\in e\quad\text{replace equals in }e\in b\\ \quad \bot\quad\text{both a statement and its negation}\\ \neg(b=e)\quad\text{close assumption 4}. \end{array} \end{equation*}
    The sign \(\bot\) records the contradiction. The label 4 lets the closing step identify exactly which assumption has been tried and rejected. We call that assumption discharged. The two facts with which we began still matter. Only the trial equality has ceased to be an open assumption.
    🔗
    We still have \(e\in e\) written on the page. This does not mean we get to keep it. It belonged to our experiment with equality, and the experiment has just failed. A checker records which assumptions each line uses. Otherwise the page would let us reject an assumption and quietly carry home its consequences. The ink survives; the permission to use that line does not.
    🔗
    We also allow the classical form of contradiction: if assuming \(\neg A\) leads to a contradiction, conclude \(A\text{.}\) This is reductio, or proof by contradiction. Equality adds two familiar moves: an object equals itself, and equal objects can replace one another in a statement. Replacing \(b\) by \(e\) was the move that exposed the contradiction in our trial.
    🔗
    There is another way an object can enter an argument temporarily: we know something exists without knowing which thing it is. Begin in the easier direction. If \(a\in b\text{,}\) then \(b\) has a member. The object \(a\) supplies an example:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a\in b}{\exists x\,(x\in b)}\quad\text{give an example}. \end{equation*}
    Read \(\exists x\) as “there exists an \(x\text{.}\)” The letter marks a place for the example in the condition. It does not give us a new particular object called \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Knowing that \(b\) has a member leaves a small but consequential blank: which member? We can use a temporary name, \(u\text{,}\) while investigating what follows. We cannot point to a favourite object already on the desk and decide that the existence statement meant that one. If we already knew that \(a\) was outside the bag, choosing \(a\) would let our own choice manufacture a contradiction. There was nothing wrong with the bag having a different member.
    🔗
    The temporary name must be fresh. It may be used within its branch, but the conclusion we carry out must not depend on which member it named. The checker scans the original existence statement, the conclusion, and the assumptions left open: the temporary name must be absent from all three. This is a condition on writing, something our worker can inspect.
    🔗
    Names also need protection when they are substituted into statements. In \(\exists x(x\in b)\text{,}\) the occurrences of \(x\) are bound by “there exists”; \(b\) is a free name for the bag. If another name for that bag happened to be \(x\text{,}\) blindly replacing \(b\) would turn the claim into “some object belongs to itself.” First change the bound letter to a fresh \(z\text{,}\) giving \(\exists z(z\in b)\text{;}\) only then replace \(b\) by its other name. We get \(\exists z(z\in x)\text{,}\) still a claim that our bag has a member. This safeguard is called avoiding capture. The workshop follows both name checks in detail.
    🔗
    The distinction will matter for our blank answer row. A proof may temporarily name a completed record and investigate its consequences. The name lets us reason about what the record would have to contain. It has not performed the missing computation.
    🔗
    Suppose a bag contains \(a\) or contains \(b\text{.}\) We can prove that it has a member without finding out which alternative holds. In the first case, offer \(a\) as the example; in the second, offer \(b\text{.}\) Either branch reaches the same conclusion. This is the same arrangement we used when a proposed repeating block either contained a 1 or contained none: follow each possibility far enough to reach the needed conclusion, then close both branches.
    🔗
    These familiar moves can be assembled from our small stock of signs. “If \(A\text{,}\) then \(B\)” forbids the case where \(A\) holds and \(B\) fails:
    \begin{equation*} A\to B\quad\text{as shorthand for}\quad \neg(A\land\neg B). \end{equation*}
    To prove it, we may temporarily assume \(A\) and derive \(B\text{.}\) Then anyone proposing \(A\land\neg B\) supplies both the premise needed for our derivation and the opposite of its result. The contradiction excludes that combination. Once the implication and \(A\) are established, the same reasoning gives \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    “\(A\) or \(B\)” excludes their both failing:
    \begin{equation*} A\lor B\quad\text{as shorthand for}\quad \neg(\neg A\land\neg B). \end{equation*}
    Either alternative can hold, and both may hold. Our bag argument fits this meaning of “or”: a bag containing both objects still has a member. The checker requires the two branches to reach the same conclusion and keeps any other assumptions they used.
    🔗
    Finally, saying that every object has a property excludes a counterexample:
    \begin{equation*} \forall x\,A(x)\quad\text{as shorthand for}\quad \neg\exists x\,\neg A(x). \end{equation*}
    The sign \(\forall\) is read “for every.” We may use a universal statement for a particular object. To prove one, we may take an arbitrary object and show the property without relying on something special about that object. “Arbitrary” is a restriction on the argument. If an open assumption says \(a\in b\text{,}\) we cannot generalize from that special information to membership of every object in \(b\text{.}\) The checker requires that the chosen name be absent from the assumptions still open.
    🔗
    The basic signs remain \(\land,\neg,\exists\text{,}\) with membership, equality, and the contradiction marker. Appendix B prints their cards and expands the convenient abbreviations. We can use the larger, familiar moves while knowing which smaller moves a checker must find underneath them.
    🔗
    Let us make one whole proof from the pieces. We know that \(b\) has a member and \(e\) has none. Give those starting statements the labels \(S,N\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} S:\ \exists x(x\in b),\qquad N:\ \forall z\neg(z\in e). \end{equation*}
    We want to show that the objects differ. Name the promised member \(u\text{,}\) then temporarily try the equality \(b=e\) inside that witness branch:
    🔗
    Line What is written Why it may be written
    1 \(\exists x(x\in b)\) Starting assumption \(S\)
    2 \(\forall z\neg(z\in e)\) Starting assumption \(N\)
    3 \([u\in b]_7\) Open the witness branch for line 1
    4 \([b=e]_8\) Try equality inside that branch
    5 \(u\in e\) Replace \(b\) by its equal \(e\) in line 3
    6 \(\neg(u\in e)\) Use line 2 for the object \(u\)
    7 \(\bot\) Lines 5 and 6 conflict
    8 \(\neg(b=e)\) Close assumption 8, using lines 4–7
    9 \(\neg(b=e)\) Close witness assumption 7, using line 1 and its branch
    Line 5 and line 6 conflict. Closing the trial equality at line 8 leaves us with \(b\ne e\text{,}\) still inside the branch for the unknown member. Line 9 carries that conclusion out. It can do so because the conclusion no longer mentions \(u\text{:}\) whichever member \(b\) has, it prevents \(b\) from being empty.
    🔗
    Follow the open assumptions once. Line 5 uses witness assumption 7 and equality assumption 8. Line 6 uses \(N\text{.}\) The contradiction therefore uses \(N,7,8\text{.}\) Closing 8 leaves \(N,7\text{;}\) closing the witness branch replaces 7 with the existence claim \(S\) that licensed it. The final dependencies are exactly \(S,N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If we tried to carry \(u\in b\) itself out at line 9, the checker would refuse. The name \(u\) is still in that proposed conclusion. It belonged to the branch, where a member could be temporarily named. Outside, the proof has established no particular object as that member.
    🔗
    At the valid ending the temporary member’s name has disappeared; at the invalid ending it has followed us out. Hilbert’s demand has become work our human computer can do: compare a conclusion with the names still permitted in it. The checker can refuse the bad ending without knowing what an empty bag is.
    🔗
    We have been talking about empty bags and adding things to them. For our records, that is nearly the whole supply cupboard. A trace starts with one configuration, acquires another, and then another. We need somewhere to collect each new piece.
    🔗
    Our set theory gives us two permissions: an empty set exists; given a set and an object, we can form a set with the old members and that object added. Adding the object is called adjunction. Together these principles form Adjunctive Set Theory, or AS. The modest inventory will have consequences much larger than its two entries suggest.
    🔗
    The empty-set assertion is built from words we now know. Nothing belongs to \(e\text{,}\) and there is such an \(e\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \exists e\,\forall z\,\neg(z\in e). \tag{Empty} \end{equation*}
    For adjunction, start with \(x\text{,}\) add \(a\text{,}\) and call the result \(b\text{.}\) An object \(z\) belongs to \(b\) precisely when it belonged to \(x\) or is the new object \(a\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \forall x\,\forall a\,\exists b\,\forall z\, \bigl(z\in b\leftrightarrow(z\in x\lor z=a)\bigr). \tag{Adjunction} \end{equation*}
    The sign \(\leftrightarrow\) means “if and only if,” the two implications joined by “and.” It both puts the specified objects in the new set and keeps all other objects out.
    🔗
    Choose an empty object \(e\text{,}\) then adjoin \(e\) to it, obtaining \(b\text{.}\) Apply the membership description to \(e\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} e\in b\leftrightarrow(e\in e\lor e=e). \end{equation*}
    The right side holds because \(e=e\text{.}\) Therefore \(e\in b\text{.}\) Here are those final moves together:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{gathered} \frac{ (e\in e\lor e=e)\to e\in b \qquad \dfrac{e=e}{e\in e\lor e=e}\ \text{or} }{e\in b}\\[3pt]\text{use if}. \end{gathered} \end{equation*}
    The implication above the left branch is supplied by adjunction. The right branch uses equality, then “or.” The last step uses the implication. Emptiness also gives \(\neg(e\in e)\text{.}\) Our earlier contradiction argument now proves \(b\ne e\) from these axioms: the empty object and this one-member object cannot be the same.
    🔗
    Add \(b\) to \(b\text{,}\) obtaining \(c\text{.}\) Its old member \(e\) stays, and its newly added member \(b\) joins it:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{e\in c\qquad b\in c} {(e\in c)\land(b\in c)}. \end{equation*}
    We already know the two members differ. The old-member side of adjunction gives the first premise; its new-member side and \(b=b\) give the second. The same rule can be used again whenever another piece needs a place.
    🔗
    For an arbitrary object \(a\text{,}\) adjoin \(a\) to an empty object. The result contains \(a\text{.}\) Give that result as the example, close its temporary witness name and the name for the empty object, then generalize on \(a\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \forall a\,\exists y\,(a\in y). \end{equation*}
    No remaining assumption singles out \(a\text{;}\) only our two closed axioms remain. The statement says every object belongs to some object. It does not require a single object containing everything.
    🔗
    AS does not say that sets with the same members are identical. That further principle is extensionality. Nor have we assumed the existence of a completed infinite set. Our two permissions make finite collections available while leaving open what else a mathematical world may contain.
    🔗
    Drop 7, 0, and 7 into one unlabelled bag. We wanted to save 707, but we have already lost it. Sets do not count a member twice, and there is no left-hand end of a bag. We must attach the positions before collecting the entries:
    \begin{equation*} \text{place 0: }7,\qquad \text{place 1: }0,\qquad \text{place 2: }7. \end{equation*}
    Each entry is an ordered pair: position first, mark second. There is a small construction that preserves this order even though the containers themselves have no order. For first item \(a\) and second item \(b\text{,}\) use adjunction to make:
    \begin{equation*} u=\{a\},\qquad v=\{a,b\},\qquad p=\{u,v\}. \end{equation*}
    The braces describe the members of chosen containers. They need not identify unique objects with those members.
    🔗
    Open the inner containers of \(p\text{.}\) The object common to all of them is \(a\text{:}\) it belongs to both, and \(u\) has no other member. That recovers the first item. Any other object in the inner containers must be \(b\text{,}\) the second item. If there is no other one, then \(b=a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For \((0,7)\text{,}\) the inner contents are \(\{0\}\) and \(\{0,7\}\text{.}\) Their common member is 0; their other member is 7. Reverse the pair and the inner contents become \(\{7\}\) and \(\{7,0\}\text{,}\) recovering 7 first. The repeated pair \((7,7)\) has only 7 in either inner container, and the recovery gives 7 twice. Even if those containers are distinct objects with the same contents, the two decoded entries remain fixed.
    🔗
    Now collect \((0,7),(1,0),(2,7)\text{.}\) The two sevens belong to different pairs, and 707 survives the trip into the bag. A configuration can travel the same way: attach each mark to its address, each card name to its address, and keep the next instruction in its designated place. Give the successive configurations their positions in the trace. Our sequence of sequences is still there, recoverable from sets whose members have no built-in order.
    🔗
    One local check can now be written completely. In entry 1 of record \(r\text{,}\) call the marked-square container \(m\text{,}\) the code for address \((0,0,0)\) by \(a\text{,}\) and the code for mark 7 by \(s\text{.}\) To say that the required pair occurs in \(m\text{,}\) write:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{split} \exists t\exists u\exists v\,\bigl(&t\in m\ \land\\ &\forall z(z\in u\leftrightarrow z=a)\ \land\\ &\forall z(z\in v\leftrightarrow(z=a\lor z=s))\ \land\\ &\forall z(z\in t\leftrightarrow(z=u\lor z=v))\bigr). \end{split} \end{equation*}
    The first line puts an entry \(t\) in \(m\text{.}\) The next three say that \(u\) has just \(a\text{,}\) \(v\) has \(a,s\text{,}\) and \(t\) has \(u,v\text{.}\) These are precisely the three containers we just opened. Build them by adjunction. For this write-7 record, form the marked-square container \(m\) by adjoining \(t\) to an empty collection. That supplies the first conjunct, \(t\in m\text{,}\) as well as the three descriptions of the inner containers. Give these objects as the examples for \(t,u,v\text{,}\) and the displayed statement follows.
    🔗
    A record that writes 9 instead fails the comparison with the distinct code for 7. The rest of the transition must be checked too: the address, next instruction, and all components that stay unchanged. A correct marked entry alone does not certify a whole calculation.
    🔗
    The searcher cannot carry a separate sentence for every possible length of computation. It needs one description saying what makes a record correct, however long that record may be. Our three-entry example has shown some of the tests; it has not constructed that uniform description. Here the book uses a general coding result. Zlatan Damnjanovic proved that adjunctive set theory, with or without extensionality, and the weak system called Robinson arithmetic can represent each other. Albert Visser’s work supplies the sequence-coding interpretation used for our records.
     1 
    Damnjanovic, “Mutual Interpretability of Robinson Arithmetic and Adjunctive Set Theory with Extensionality,” Bulletin of Symbolic Logic 23 (2017), 381–404, doi:10.1017/bsl.2017.30. The arithmetic and sequence properties required here are specified in Appendix B, with Visser’s references. The full interpretation is not constructed in this book.
    🔗
    An interpretation supplies formulas identifying which objects represent numbers and records, how their entries are read, and when different codes represent the same thing. Think of the positions in 707: being a bag was not enough to make an object a position, and being a member of the bag was not enough to say which digit came first. The translated arithmetic and sequence laws supply those distinctions uniformly, with proofs in AS. The workshop gives a small example of represented numbers and represented equality. Our adopted axioms remain Empty and Adjunction.
    🔗
    Fix such a representation once, including its formulas for instruction texts and output words. The property needed immediately is this: each actual finite successful computation can be turned mechanically into a proof of its output sentence. The local checks on \(r\) become pieces of that proof. Longer supplied records require more pieces, but the translation finishes for each one. Call this the finite-trace property. It is a requirement on our coding of computation, justified through the interpretation; merely putting three entries into containers did not prove it.
    🔗
    Return to the three empty answer places beside our searcher. If it ever writes 314 and finishes, the completed work can be converted into a proof that it did so. The conversion may use a dismaying amount of paper to certify three digits. Length will not save the certificate that provoked the response: however long the confirming proof, it would still be a proof of the result the first certificate excluded.
    🔗
    Our checker begins at the leaves of a proposed proof tree. It matches rule patterns, checks substitutions and fresh names, and carries the open assumptions toward the conclusion. A misplaced symbol can spoil a step: from \(e\in c\) and \(b\in c\text{,}\) the conjunction \((e\in c)\land(c\in b)\) fails. The surrounding mathematical intention cannot repair the reversed names.
    🔗
    The checker eventually finishes with each proposed finite page. Producing a page worth checking is harder. We could wait for inspiration, but the searcher has no instructions for that. It can use the extravagant alternative of trying every finite string. Our old ordering puts every candidate somewhere within reach. For a two-symbol alphabet it begins:
    \begin{equation*} 0,\ 1;\qquad 00,\ 01,\ 10,\ 11;\qquad 000,\ 001,\ 010,\ldots. \end{equation*}
    Use the proof alphabet instead. Names such as v0 and v17 give us indefinitely many variables written with finitely many symbols. Most pages will be gibberish. Among the grammatical pages, most will be wrong. The worker checks and continues, undiscouraged by this spectacular lack of literary progress. Every genuine finite proof nevertheless has a finite place in the list. Eventually its page comes up.
    🔗
    If a requested sentence is provable, the search eventually finds it. If not, each inspection still finishes, but the search does not. Searching simultaneously for a statement and its negation does not guarantee relief: neither may have a proof.
    🔗
    The rules may include an endless mechanically generated list of extra axioms. A proof using one attaches a finite record of the axiom-printer reaching it. The checker can verify that attachment; it need not decide whether an absent axiom will someday appear. This keeps the search available for many different proof methods.
    🔗
    Our waiting program can read this stream of checked proofs as each one arrives. A conclusion that would make a mathematician put down the pencil may make this program pick it up. The worker compares the conclusion with a fixed pattern. If the pattern matches, its next instructions tell it what to write.
    🔗
    Allow any finite digit word as the result, not just 314. The program looks for a proof saying that this very program cannot finish with a particular word. On finding one, it returns the excluded word and halts. A proof excluding 0007 would provoke all four symbols, including the zeros. At this point the result is a word, not its value as an integer.
    🔗
    Kleene’s construction supplies the complete description at SELF. NextProof continues the enumeration until it reaches another checked certificate. ExcludedWord compares that certificate’s conclusion with the sentence pattern about SELF, returning Y and the excluded word when it matches, or N otherwise. Copy performs the promised response:
    🔗
    Search: Call NextProof(proof)
Call ExcludedWord(proof, SELF, answer, word)
If(answer,N) Goto Search
Call Copy(word, RESULT)
Halt
    We can prepare all these directions while leaving open whether the desired certificate will ever appear. ExcludedWord recognizes a written form; it makes no discovery about the conclusion’s truth. The response has the same simplicity as copying 314 from one row to another. The mathematical difficulty lies in whether a checked proof could ever give the response something to copy.
    🔗
    Before starting the search, fix the proof method, called \(T\text{,}\) and fix how an output claim is written. Given program text \(e\) and a finite digit word \(w\text{,}\) the sentence-making directions produce \(\operatorname{Out}(e,w)\text{:}\) there is a correct completed record of that program ending with that word. The directions must finish, must express that claim correctly about ordinary finite records, and must leave \(w\) recoverable when the searcher recognizes the sentence. The proof checker and searcher use the same convention.
    🔗
    Assume that \(T\) uses our classical first-order rules, that its certificates can be mechanically enumerated, and that every actual finite success can effectively be certified in \(T\) by this fixed output sentence. Assume also that \(T\) is consistent: it does not prove both a statement and its negation. AS with the fixed coding just described is the intended example. Another proof method meeting these requirements gives another searcher; consistency by itself would not give the worker a language for recognizing results.
    🔗
    Suppose the search finds the desired kind of certificate. There is a first one in its search order. Say it excludes 314. The program returns 314. Its search as far as that certificate, its checking, and its three final writes constitute an actual finite calculation. By the finite-trace property, that calculation gives \(T\) a proof that it returns 314.
    🔗
    The same method now certifies both the exclusion and the excluded result. Consistency forbids that pair. Our supposition must be rejected: the search never finds such a certificate. If the first excluded word had been 0007, the response would have certified 0007 instead. The argument has not depended on which word appeared first.
    🔗
    Call the complete program \(E\text{.}\) The output sentence now concerns its own description, supplied by Kleene’s construction. For every particular finite word,
    \begin{equation*} T\text{ does not prove }\neg\operatorname{Out}(E,w). \end{equation*}
    At our desk, \(E\) searches forever. The answer row stays blank. Yet ask \(T\) to rule out any particular finite result—314, 0007, or any word we choose—and no excluding proof is available.
    🔗
    We have reached the point where two familiar assurances part company. Consistency says that the proof method never certifies a statement and its negation. Truth about our ordinary worker asks whether the record a sentence describes can actually be finished on our paper. The argument has used a bridge in one direction: an actual finite success can be certified. We have never assumed that every certified assertion of existence comes with an ordinary successful computation. The difference matters precisely where the answer row remains blank.
    🔗
    Consider adding “\(E\) returns 314” to \(T\text{.}\) If the addition led to contradiction, the contradicting branch could be closed by our negation rule, yielding an old \(T\)-proof that this output is impossible. We have just proved there is no such proof. The extra assertion can therefore be added consistently.
    🔗
    The same argument works for 0007 or any other particular finite word. Each choice is considered separately. Putting “returns 314” beside “returns 0007” is a different demand from letting a longer decimal beginning retain an earlier one. We will need that second kind of compatibility when long division returns to the story.
    🔗
    A simpler version makes the limit of proof especially plain. Give a new searcher, \(J\text{,}\) just one sentence to look for: “\(J\) never halts.” If it finds a proof, it halts. Kleene makes the description in that sentence the description of this complete searcher. There is no other exit.
    🔗
    If \(T\) proved that \(J\) never halts, the search would reach the proof and halt. Its completed computation would supply the opposite proof. Consistency therefore rules out the searched-for certificate.
    🔗
    From outside the search, we can finish the reasoning. The proof is absent, and finding it was the only way to stop, so \(J\) runs forever. The sentence it seeks is true and unprovable in \(T\text{.}\) The worker has not gone wrong or misunderstood a word. It could continue in perfect obedience for as long as we watched. What it cannot receive is the particular proof that would allow it to stop obeying the search loop.
    🔗
    Our conclusion has one direction: the true statement that \(J\) never halts has no proof in \(T\text{.}\) Consistency alone has not ruled out a proof of its opposite; it is weaker than truth about ordinary computations. Rosser’s refinement obtains a sentence with neither side provable under the appropriate hypotheses. That stronger result, and the separate theorem about proving a theory’s own consistency, are not needed for the missing 314 record we are about to investigate.
    🔗
    Gödel first revealed the incompleteness obstruction. Our route through Turing’s worker and Kleene’s self-description is a later route into it, rather than its historical discovery. Hilbert’s demand has made proof precise enough to be read by a program, and the program can turn what it reads into action. Church’s work on symbolic calculation and Kleene’s construction meet here, on the same piece of paper.
    🔗
    Return to the earlier searcher that responds with an excluded word. Leave it running, and write beside it: “It returns 314.” We can add that assertion consistently, even though the ordinary execution never reaches those digits. We know what a completed record would have to say: the right beginning, each instructed change, the three final writes. Merely wishing for the ending cannot fill in the intervening pages.
    🔗
    Leon Henkin’s construction begins with something smaller than those missing pages. It gives the promised record a name. We are about to see how much a name can commit a world to contain.
