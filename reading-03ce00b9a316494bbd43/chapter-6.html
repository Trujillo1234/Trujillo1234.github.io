    Chapter 6 A world for the words

    Leave the search running. On a second page write, “It finishes with 314.” The first page keeps failing to supply the digits; the second has acquired a promise of them. We proved that the promise can be added to our axioms consistently, and also that no amount of ordinary waiting will make this search fulfill it.
    🔗
    We could put 314 at the bottom ourselves, but the checker would ask for the intervening configurations. Every step must follow its instruction. The claim asks for a whole completed record, and our hands cannot finish supplying one.
    🔗
    Usually we have objects in front of us and ask whether a sentence describes them correctly. Leon Henkin worked in the other direction. Starting with consistent sentences, he built objects for them to describe. The promise of a completed record would have to acquire a setting in which it could be kept.
    🔗
    Henkin was another of Alonzo Church’s doctoral students. His doctorate was completed in 1947; the completeness proof appeared in 1949. Gödel had proved completeness earlier, but Henkin’s construction made names on a page into the materials of a world. In his later account of the discovery, he credited Church’s teaching and writing with the main ideas that fed the proof. We have followed Church’s students from instructions, through instructions about themselves, to the objects those instructions can be said to concern.
    🔗
    The setting must make all the starting sentences true together, using one collection of objects and one interpretation of their relations. Such a setting is a model. We cannot give the sentence about 314 its own accommodating meaning while leaving all the other sentences elsewhere. Every claim must live with the others.
    🔗
    The missing computation is a complicated place to start. A bag with an unnamed member will let us see the work at a smaller scale.
    🔗
    Our notebook has made one promise:
    \begin{equation*} \exists x\,(x\in b). \end{equation*}
    Read it aloud: “There is something in \(b\text{.}\)” No particular member has yet been named. Choose a new name, say \(c\text{,}\) and add
    \begin{equation*} c\in b. \end{equation*}
    We keep the name \(c\text{.}\) In our earlier proof a witness name belonged to a temporary branch; here the name and the membership claim remain in the notebook as requirements for the world we are building. We have given the promised member a way to be mentioned again.
    🔗
    A new name leaves room without changing an old promise. Suppose we already know \(a\notin b\text{.}\) Calling the promised member \(a\) would immediately contradict that entry, though some other object could belong to \(b\) quite happily. The contradiction would be our contribution. A name with no previous commitments avoids it.
    🔗
    Our eventual notebook will contain many existence statements. We prepare for them by adding conditional promises. For an assertion \(\exists x\,A(x)\text{,}\) reserve a fresh name \(c\) and write
    \begin{equation*} \text{If there is an }x\text{ with }A(x),\text{ then }A(c). \end{equation*}
    Write it compactly as \((\exists x\,A(x))\to A(c)\text{.}\) Here \(A(x)\) stands for the condition, such as “\(x\) is a member of \(b\text{.}\)” If nothing meets the condition, our promise asks nothing of \(c\text{.}\) If something does meet it, \(c\) will name an example. We can reserve a name before we know which of these situations the completed notebook will describe.
    🔗
    We can check the promise with the proof moves already on our cards. Use the particular promise about membership in \(b\text{,}\) and suppose adding it caused a contradiction. Closing that temporary assumption would give the old notebook a proof that the promise is false. What would make “if something belongs to \(b\text{,}\) then \(c\) belongs to \(b\)” false? Its first part would have to hold and its second part fail. In symbols, our old notebook would prove both
    \begin{equation*} \exists x\,(x\in b)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad \neg(c\in b). \end{equation*}
    This step uses our definition of “if”: the only forbidden case is a true first part together with a false second part.
    🔗
    But the remaining assumptions are all old notebook entries. None mentions \(c\text{.}\) The proof of \(\neg(c\in b)\) has therefore used nothing special about this fresh name. The arbitrary-example rule gives
    \begin{equation*} \forall x\,\neg(x\in b). \end{equation*}
    The first displayed existence statement lets us temporarily name a member \(d\text{,}\) so \(d\in b\text{.}\) The assertion about every object gives \(\neg(d\in b)\text{.}\) These are opposites. Close the temporary witness branch: the contradiction no longer mentions \(d\text{.}\) It was already obtainable from the old notebook alone. If that notebook was consistent, adding our fresh-name promise cannot have caused the supposed trouble.
    🔗
    Replace “\(x\in b\)” throughout by any condition \(A(x)\text{,}\) and the same proof goes through. The crucial moment is the move from the fresh \(c\) to an arbitrary object. It is permitted because, after the trial promise is discharged, no remaining assumption singles out that name.
    🔗
    There is another sort of gap to fill. Perhaps the notebook has not settled whether \(a\in b\text{.}\) Write that question on a card. We may add either “\(a\in b\)” or “\(a\notin b\text{,}\)” provided the choice does not produce a contradiction.
    🔗
    Both choices cannot be forbidden. If assuming \(a\in b\) leads to contradiction, our rejection rule gives \(a\notin b\text{.}\) If assuming \(a\notin b\) also leads to contradiction, classical reductio gives \(a\in b\text{.}\) Both failures together would put a statement and its opposite in the old notebook. A consistent notebook therefore has at least one safe continuation. The same two branches work for any sentence we put in place of \(a\in b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Existence of a safe choice does not give our worker a way to choose it. A proof search can expose a contradiction if a contradiction is there to be found. After a long unsuccessful search, the worker still has only the familiar result: no proof yet. We have proved that a consistent direction exists at every stage, without supplying an infallible signpost toward it. As with Baire’s escape, the choices in this existence argument may be beyond a finite instruction sheet.
    🔗
    There are now two kinds of unfinished business: unanswered questions and unnamed witnesses. Our starting collection of assumptions is called \(T\text{.}\) Its written language is countable: its finite expressions can be placed in a numbered list. Reserve an endless numbered list of new names, none used by \(T\text{.}\) Make one list of sentence-decision jobs and another of existence-promise jobs, allowing those new names in both. Alternate between the lists. At a decision job, choose a consistent answer. At a promise job, use a reserve name that has not yet occurred. Even if \(T\) has infinitely many assumptions, they use none of our reserve names. Only finitely many reserve names have been used at any finite stage of the new work, so another is available.
    🔗
    Keep the little statement about a member of \(b\) in view. Suppose our starting assumptions allow \(a\in b\) and \(a\ne b\) together. These two commitments are compatible: an empty set and a set containing it provide one example. A few entries in a possible notebook then look like this:
    🔗
    question or promise what the notebook receives
    Does \(a\in b\text{?}\) \(a\in b\)
    Are \(a\) and \(b\) different? \(a\ne b\)
    If \(b\) has a member \((\exists x\,x\in b)\to c\in b\text{,}\) with \(c\) fresh
    Does \(c=a\text{?}\) \(c=a\)
    The first entry already ensures that \(b\) has a member, so the promise gives \(c\in b\text{.}\) Then the last decision lets \(c\) name the same member as \(a\text{.}\) We have added a name without adding another object. Fresh ink does not require fresh furniture. That equality is compatible with the entries in this example; different starting assumptions could require the names to remain distinct.
    🔗
    The complete schedule eventually attends to every sentence, including ones containing newly supplied names, and to every existence statement with those names as parameters. Listing all finite strings in the enlarged alphabet provides such a schedule.
    🔗
    There is a worry about keeping so many promises. Perhaps each finite stage is consistent, but the whole notebook somehow becomes too much. Any contradiction would still need a proof, however, and a proof tree is a finite drawing. It could use only finitely many of our new entries. All of those would have been present at some finite stage, a stage we kept consistent. A contradiction cannot wait until the end of an infinite notebook and then conceal which pages it used.
    🔗
    Call the completed notebook, including all its provable consequences, \(K\text{.}\) Each sentence has received an answer without contradiction; each promised object has a name available for it. We can now stop adding writing and see what the writing supplies.
    🔗
    The notebook now settles every sentence, and we still have not supplied its objects. We have supplied a great many names. Henkin’s construction uses what is already on the page.
    🔗
    First put together the names that the notebook says are equal. If it says \(a=c\) and \(c=e\text{,}\) the equality rules also give \(a=e\text{,}\) so all three belong together. Each name goes with itself, and reversing an equality changes nothing about the group. Make one object for each group. Such a group is called an equivalence class: all its names will point to the same object.
    🔗
    This grouping keeps our little notebook’s promise that \(c=a\text{.}\) Treating each name as a different object would have broken that promise at once. “Six” and “half a dozen” do not double the number of eggs.
    🔗
    Try that grouping on the names in our little notebook. Of the three names shown, \(a\) and \(c\) go together, while \(b\) stays separate:
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{\{a,c\}}_{\text{names of one object}} \quad\xrightarrow{\quad\text{is a member of}\quad}\quad \underbrace{\{b\}}_{\text{names of another object}}. \end{equation*}
    The arrow records the entry \(a\in b\text{,}\) which is also \(c\in b\text{.}\) Look back at our first existence claim: does \(b\) have a member? The object on the left is the promised example. The two names on it give two ways to point to the same example.
    🔗
    We are seeing a small fragment: three names, two groups, and one known link. Our whole set theory still needs its further names and membership decisions; these two objects alone do not give a model of it. Even in the fragment, the earlier writing constrains what we may do. The entry \(a\ne b\) prevents us from merging the two displayed objects later.
    🔗
    To see exactly what the arrow supplies, call the left object \(U\) and the right object \(V\text{.}\) These are labels for our two groups. The information supplied so far makes this partial table:
    🔗
    is a member of \(U\text{,}\) named by \(a,c\) \(V\text{,}\) named by \(b\)
    \(U\text{,}\) named by \(a,c\) not yet recorded yes
    \(V\text{,}\) named by \(b\) not yet recorded not yet recorded
    Read across \(U\)’s row. The “yes” says that \(U\) belongs to \(V\text{,}\) answering both \(a\in b\) and \(c\in b\text{.}\) It also provides our promised example for \(\exists x(x\in b)\text{:}\) take \(U\) as \(x\text{,}\) and the table says yes.
    🔗
    There are two kinds of membership we must keep apart here. The printed letters \(a,c\) belong to the group of names we used to make \(U\text{.}\) The table tells us where \(U\) itself belongs in the world we are building. Looking inside the printed braces cannot answer the second question. Nor can a blank in this partial table: an unrecorded entry still awaits a decision.
    🔗
    The completed notebook gives us the whole picture. If \(K\) contains \(a\in b\text{,}\) draw a membership link from the object named by \(a\) to the object named by \(b\text{.}\) If it contains \(a\notin b\text{,}\) leave that membership link out. These links define the membership relation between our objects. We are not asserting that the groups of names themselves have ordinary set membership arranged this way. We are specifying the relation that the sign \(\in\) will mean in our constructed setting. If \(c=a\text{,}\) equality substitution makes the decision for \(c\in b\) the same as the decision for \(a\in b\text{.}\) Changing a label therefore cannot change the membership relation.
    🔗
    If \([a]\) means the group of names equal to \(a\text{,}\) our rule is
    \begin{equation*} [a]\in[b]\quad\text{when the notebook contains }a\in b. \end{equation*}
    Here the brackets group equal names. They have a different job from the brackets around a decimal drawer: they collect all the names of one object. Our present set language needs only names and membership. If we later add a symbol for an operation, an expression naming its result can be treated as a name too; the same equality checks still apply.
    🔗
    The names were on a numbered list. Replacing each name by its group gives a list of every object in our model, perhaps with repetitions. Thus the model is countable from outside. This fact will matter when we return to Skolem’s observation.
    🔗
    So far we have arranged for the names, equalities, and membership links to agree with the notebook. What about a whole sentence assembled from them? The little words we learned in the last chapter make the check possible. Start with a conjunction such as
    \begin{equation*} (a\in b)\wedge(c\in b), \end{equation*}
    the notebook contains it exactly when it contains both parts. This follows from the two conjunction rules: take the parts out, or put them together. Our constructed objects therefore make the conjunction true exactly when the notebook contains it.
    🔗
    For a negation, the decision jobs put one of \(A\) and \(\neg A\) in the notebook, and consistency prevents both. If the smaller claim \(A\) agrees with our objects, its negation therefore agrees too: \(\neg A\) is in the notebook precisely when \(A\) is false in the world we have constructed.
    🔗
    Existence is the step we tried in the small picture: the entry \(\exists x\,(x\in b)\) supplied the witness name \(c\text{,}\) and its group supplied an actual member of the object named by \(b\text{.}\) For any existence statement the check is the same. If the notebook says \(\exists x A(x)\text{,}\) its promise supplies \(A(c)\text{.}\) The object named by \(c\) is an example. In the other direction, suppose an object in our constructed world is an example. Every object was made from names, so choose one of its names, \(t\text{.}\) The smaller claim \(A(t)\) has already passed our comparison between the notebook and its objects, so \(A(t)\) is in the notebook. The rule “give an example” puts \(\exists x A(x)\) there too.
    🔗
    A long sentence can nest these operations many times, but its writing is finite. Begin with its simplest claims, whose truth was fixed by construction, and work outward. Each new layer agrees with the notebook because its smaller pieces already agree. This proves that every sentence is true in the constructed world exactly when it is in \(K\text{.}\) The verification is called the truth lemma.
    🔗
    Our larger vocabulary comes along with it. “Or,” “if,” and “every” were abbreviations using the same three operations, so we have checked them too. The notebook has acquired objects that answer to all its sentences, including the complicated ones for which we could never draw a useful bag.
    🔗
    Our language is called first-order. Its “there is” and “every” ask about objects in the model we are building. In the membership example, \(\exists x(x\in b)\) asks whether some one of those objects belongs to the object named by \(b\text{.}\) A universal statement asks about each object in that same collection. The variables do not automatically range over every collection we can imagine from outside the model. When our objects are sets, a set among the model’s objects may itself be chosen for \(x\text{;}\) that still does not put every externally possible collection into the model. This is the range of questions for which our construction has supplied the answers.
    🔗
    In particular, all our starting assumptions are true in this world. We have proved Henkin’s model-existence theorem:
    🔗
    Every consistent first-order collection of assumptions in a countable language has a model.
    🔗
    🔗
    Completeness here concerns proof and interpretation. It is a different use of the word from the completeness of the real line behind Baire’s nested intervals.
    🔗
    A proof has a last line. It also has only finitely many lines. This small fact gives Henkin’s construction a remarkable reach. Suppose every finite selection of requests can be met in some model. Could all the requests together be contradictory? Any proof of contradiction would be a finite tree using only finitely many of them. Their model would make the assumptions true; each valid proof step preserves truth; so the contradiction at the bottom would have to be true. That is impossible. There is no such tree. Henkin therefore gives a model meeting the entire collection together.
    🔗
    This is the compactness theorem: if every finite selection of our requests has a model, the whole countable collection has one. The individual selections may have quite different models. What brings them together is the inability of a finite proof to use infinitely many premises.
    🔗
    Ask someone to supply more pages than a finite list requests. Ten pages? Bring eleven. A million? The practical arrangements become unpleasant, but the arithmetic remains easy: take more than the largest number on the list. Now keep the same name for the number of pages while extending the list without end. Use \(b\) for that integer size and write
    \begin{equation*} b>0,\qquad b>1,\qquad b>2,\qquad b>3,\qquad\ldots \end{equation*}
    For this illustration, keep the true first-order statements about ordinary integer arithmetic, with its order, addition, and multiplication, alongside the new requests. If someone takes only the first four requests, \(b=4\) will do. If they take requests up to \(b>1000\text{,}\) use \(1001\text{.}\) Even a selection with large gaps has a largest mentioned ordinary numeral, and a larger ordinary integer works. Every finite selection can be met in the ordinary integers. Compactness gives one model in which all the requests hold.
    🔗
    The wording of the requests determines what we have obtained. Each one names an ordinary numeral: zero, one, two, and so on. None puts \(b\) on the right-hand side. Compare that list with the single sentence \(\forall x\,(b>x)\text{,}\) “\(b\) is larger than every integer in this model.” That sentence would also demand \(b>b\text{.}\) The ordering rules forbid it. Our separate requests make no such demand. Their right-hand sides run through ordinary numerals, while a first-order “every” ranges over all the objects in the model. The difference in writing leaves room for a difference in worlds.
    🔗
    There is no ordinary integer larger than every ordinary integer. The model’s object \(b\) is consequently an extra integer-like object beyond all the ordinary numerals. It is called nonstandard. The model also has \(b+1\text{,}\) and still larger integers beyond it. The finite selections had ordinary witnesses; their common model has a larger domain of possible witnesses.
    🔗
    There is no first extra integer waiting immediately after all the ordinary ones. Here we are using integer arithmetic with its usual successor and ordering laws: every positive integer has a predecessor, and subtracting one reverses adding one. If \(b\) exceeds every ordinary numeral, take any such numeral \(n\text{.}\) Our requests include \(b>n+1\text{,}\) so those laws give \(b-1>n\text{.}\) Thus \(b-1\) also exceeds every ordinary numeral, and it is smaller than \(b\text{.}\) The same reasoning applies to \(b-2\) and \(b-3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On a page we can write \(0,1,2,3\text{,}\) then farther down write \(b-2,b-1,b,b+1\text{.}\) Put a finger beside the first row and slide it to the second. A moment’s work. The ink has made the two neighborhoods look conveniently close.
    🔗
    We have drawn two finite windows into the model’s arithmetic. The blank between them is no map of what lies between. It conceals no last ordinary integer that we forgot to label; there is no last one. Every ordinary counting step we finish has another ordinary step after it. The page lets us point to \(b\text{,}\) but it has supplied no sequence of ordinary counting steps that reaches \(b\text{.}\) Our finger has crossed a piece of paper.
    🔗
    Our coded finite records can behave the same way. Ask for one record with more than one line, more than two, more than three, and so on. A model can regard that record as finite while its length exceeds every ordinary numeral. We can name its last line as readily as we wrote \(b\text{.}\) Naming it will not help the worker arrive there. “Finite in the model” and “finished after an ordinary finite number of steps” have ceased to make the same demand.
    🔗
    Return to the promised 314. Chapter 5 showed that the assertion of this output can be added consistently, although the ordinary search never finds the forbidden certificate. Henkin supplies a model of that enlarged collection. Its interpretation of our output sentence supplies a completed record, including a last configuration.
    🔗
    Here we need a further feature of the fixed representation of records. Its laws must fix the decoded starting configuration and give each nonhalting configuration exactly the decoded successor required by the instruction. A write changes its specified mark; a move changes its specified place card; every other component stays put. These requirements are set out in Appendix B. They concern what the record formulas say in models, beyond the earlier promise that actual successful computations receive proofs.
    🔗
    Apply those laws once, and the model’s second configuration agrees with the worker’s actual second configuration. Apply them again for the third. Repeating the check five times, or a million times, fixes exactly that many ordinary steps. None reaches a halt. The model’s completed record must therefore end beyond every ordinary stage. The instructions have not changed; the objects over which “there is a finite record” ranges include records we cannot finish writing.
    🔗
    The search, however, has only one exit. To finish with 314, the model must supply a candidate exclusion proof, a record of its acceptance by the checker, and the searcher’s return of those three digits. Could we bring that certificate back to our desk? If it were an ordinary finite proof, we could enumerate as far as it, run the response, and produce the opposite proof ourselves. We would recover exactly the contradiction from Chapter 5, so consistency rules out such an ordinary certificate. The model’s account of finding one leaves the ordinary search running.
    🔗
    Our division worker is still at the desk, with its subtraction rules and its way of bringing down a zero. Let two programs write its numerator and denominator. A completed record for each would let division begin, but separate promises are not enough for the destination we have in mind. We will keep the two results and their division in one record, then ask the same record for a first digit, a second, and each further digit we choose. Henkin has supplied a way to turn compatible requests into a world. Long division will supply the requests.
