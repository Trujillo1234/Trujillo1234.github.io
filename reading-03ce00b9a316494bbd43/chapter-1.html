    Chapter 1 Long division at the desk

    The computer has gone home. On the desk are a pencil, several pages of figures, and a calculation waiting for the next shift. This was once an ordinary arrangement: a computer was a person employed to calculate. At the Langley laboratory in 1935, women calculated from experimental data, plotted results, and checked the work that went back to engineers. They also developed methods. The job required judgment, care, and a person who could sometimes notice that the instructions were wrong.
     1 
    NASA’s historical account, “When the Computer Wore a Skirt: Langley’s Computers, 1935–1970,” describes the group. In 1935 the laboratory belonged to NACA.
    🔗
    We are going to take that person’s chair, with one peculiar condition. The calculation must be able to continue even when the person taking over knows nothing about its purpose. Whatever understanding is needed to choose the next action must somehow be left on the desk. Turing began his investigation of computation with a person in just this situation: following definite rules, making marks, attending to one little part of the work at a time. Our first task for this person is long division.
    🔗
    Here is the work so far:
    🔗
    Eight goes into twelve once, leaving four. Bring down the three and we have forty-three; five eights leave three. The answer is fifteen, with three left over. There is nothing unusual on the page yet, except that we intend to be rather nosy about what happens next.
    🔗
    Put the decimal point after fifteen. Writing the original number as \(123.000\ldots\) gives us zeros to bring down. The first joins the remainder three, making thirty. An experienced worker sees that the next answer digit is three. Suppose we leave that little flash of recognition out of the instructions. The paper can find the digit for us:
    \begin{equation*} 30\longrightarrow22\longrightarrow14\longrightarrow6. \end{equation*}
    Take away eight, and then eight again, and then another eight. A fourth eight will not fit. Three subtractions succeeded, so three belongs after the decimal point; six remains for the following round.
    🔗
    With eight, this feels like taking the stairs to a place we could reach in one step. A divisor of \(237\) is more persuasive. How many copies fit into \(1538\text{?}\) Successive subtractions leave \(1301,1064,827,590,353,116\text{:}\) six copies fit, and a seventh does not. The little flash of recognition has become scratch work. It is slower, perhaps, but another person can pick it up where we left it.
    🔗
    Beside our own scratch work, reserve a square for the count. It starts at zero, becomes one after the first successful subtraction, then two, then three. The failure must leave it at three. Ordinary subtraction would let us continue \(6-8\) into negative numbers and write \(-2\text{.}\) For division, the useful news is that another eight does not fit. We need two ways out of the subtraction: one carrying a new remainder, the other sending us back to the last remainder we accepted.
    🔗
    That decision will eventually have to be made by marks on the paper. To see how much a few marks can do, take the addition \(587+468\text{.}\) It contains the same small acts—look at a column, write below it, remember something for the column to its left—without the failed trial getting in the way.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{r@{\,}rrrr} &\scriptstyle1&\scriptstyle1&\scriptstyle1&\\ &&5&8&7\\ +&&4&6&8\\\hline &1&0&5&5 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Seven and eight make fifteen. Five goes under the line and one goes above the next column. There it waits for us. Eight and six, with that one, make fifteen again. Another five, another carried one. Five and four and one make ten, giving the final zero and one of \(1055\text{.}\) The worker has remembered the rules of addition. The page has remembered which column owes us a one.
    🔗
    Give each digit a square of graph paper and rest a finger in the carry row above the units. The two digits lie one and two squares below it; the answer belongs three squares below. The same arrangement will hold when the finger travels left. Here are the first two moments, just before their columns are worked:
    🔗
    The second picture is what obeying the first picture’s card leaves behind. We have written a five and a one, and moved our attention. Everything else stayed where it was. Do the next column and the attention arrives above the five. Do that one and it arrives over two empty squares, with a carried one still waiting:
    🔗
    The empty squares have supplied information too: both numbers have ended. We put the carried one below the line and finish. In \(12+3\text{,}\) one number runs out before the other. Above the blank to the left of the three sits the one from twelve; that column still has work to do. The instruction must distinguish one blank from two.
    🔗
    Now suppose the sheet ends immediately to the left of our five. The final carried one has nowhere to sit. A second sheet, with its squares lined up against the first, supplies the missing column. We have changed the amount of paper, without changing what “one square left” means. The same repair works at the other edges. Sheets can meet along sides and around corners, like this:
    🔗
    The small gaps let us see the individual sheets; on the desk the squares join. When this larger patch runs out, more sheets can be attached. We allow the supply to continue for as long as the work requires. At any particular moment there are only finitely many marks and sheets, but we have declined to settle in advance where the last edge will be.
    🔗
    The addition has used four particular cards. To cover any addition, we need all the possible column facts. Put them into two tables, one for a column with a carry arriving and one without. The upper digit picks the row; the lower picks the column. Each entry says what to write below and whether to leave a carry on the left. Blank has its own row and column. Ten digits and blank give eleven observations in each number square: \(11\times11=121\) entries per table, \(242\) altogether. The tables have room for a million-digit addition without becoming million-digit tables. Its columns will keep asking the same small collection of questions.
    🔗
    Take those questions back to \(30-8\text{,}\) the first subtraction still waiting in our division. Zero cannot supply eight, so we borrow a ten. Ten minus eight gives two; the three tens have become two tens:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{r@{\,}rr} &\scriptstyle2&\scriptstyle10\\ &3&0\\-& &8\\\hline&2&2 \end{array} \qquad 30-8=22. \end{equation*}
    There is another way to leave the borrowing reminder. Keep the three unchanged and put a one above its column: “subtract one extra here; the column on the right has borrowed it.” We can then read the original numbers throughout the trial. Subtraction gets two tables of its own, depending on whether a borrow is arriving. Our two columns use these entries:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccc|cc} \text{upper}&\text{lower}&\text{borrow in}&\text{write}&\text{borrow left}\\\hline 0&8&\text{blank}&2&1\\ 3&\text{blank}&1&2&\text{blank} \end{array} \end{equation*}
    At the next step left, both numbers have ended and nothing is owed. The subtraction succeeds. Twenty-two can become our next remainder.
    🔗
    In the last trial, \(6-8\text{,}\) the units column borrows a ten and temporarily writes eight. Move left: two blanks, and the borrowed ten has nowhere to come from. This entry takes the other exit. The eight we wrote is scratch work from a failed trial, not a strangely optimistic answer to \(6-8\text{.}\) The worker need not recognize failure by looking at the whole subtraction. It is already there in the two blanks and the unpaid borrow.
    🔗
    For \(8-8\text{,}\) the units entry writes zero without borrowing. The next two blanks report success. One eight fitted exactly, and we must count it before trying \(0-8\) and meeting the refusal. Both tables include their blank cases, giving the same \(242\) possibilities as addition. Long rows only repeat these local choices. In \(1000-1\text{,}\) each zero passes the borrow left; the one pays it, and the answer row says \(0999\text{.}\) We will tidy that extra zero shortly.
    🔗
    Our arithmetic is ready to travel, but the places are becoming a nuisance. One finger is holding the answer square after \(15.\text{.}\) Others want to follow the two numbers and the trial answer. The instruction “put it back there” is beginning to ask a lot of there. Give that square an address. Call the original working surface page zero and the answer row row zero. Put the first answer digit in column zero, with column numbers increasing to the right. Then the square after \(15.\) has address page zero, column three, row zero:
    \begin{equation*} \texttt{OUT}:\quad(0,3,0). \end{equation*}
    Write it on a card labeled OUT. The label says what the card is for; its three numbers say where to put the pencil. Rows increase upward, and negative row or column numbers continue below or to the left of our chosen starting square. A place card can hold a place while its owner’s attention goes elsewhere.
    🔗
    The joined sheets make one expandable page. For a subtraction trial, we want a different page, where a mistake cannot overwrite the remainder we still need. Call the first scratch surface page one, the next page two, and so on. Keep the same column and row numbers on each. The first trial leaves twenty-two on page one; the next leaves fourteen on page two. Arrange their answer units at column one, row zero. The one of fourteen is then at \((2,0,0)\text{,}\) the four at \((2,1,0)\text{.}\) A new trial can copy them to page three.
    🔗
    Here is the whole copying journey. A card A begins at the one on page two; B begins at \((3,0,2)\text{,}\) an empty square on page three. Read one at A, write one at B, move both places right. Read four, write four, move both right again. A now finds blank, which ends the copy. Fourteen has arrived on the new page without disappearing from the old one. Ten branches cover the possible digits; an eleventh covers blank. The one-branch says to write one, the four-branch to write four, and every digit branch makes the same two moves before looking again.
    🔗
    We also need to put eight under the four, not under the one. Start at the first copied digit, walk right to blank, and step back once. That is the units square. Save it, then move down one row and write eight. For a longer divisor, begin with its units digit too and copy leftward from there. Lining up the ends aligns all the columns, even when the numbers have different lengths.
    🔗
    These directions are acquiring a compact written form. If C holds an answer place, Write(C,5) puts a five there. Move(C,0,-1,0) moves that place one column left: zero change of page, minus one column, zero change of row. The five does not move. Place(U,C) copies the address onto another card, U, so that C can go away and later return by following Place(C,U). Saving a place copies its address, not the digit at that address.
    🔗
    For the arithmetic choice, If(A,7) asks whether the upper square contains seven. A successful test can send us to another labeled line by Goto. Here is the seven-plus-eight branch from our addition, with A and B at the two digits, C below them at the answer, and D in the blank carry square above:
    🔗
    pair78: If(A,7) Goto check8
    Goto other-pairs
    check8: If(B,8) Goto write5
    Goto other-pairs
    write5: Write(C,5)
    Move(D,0,-1,0)
    Write(D,1)
    Move(A,0,-1,0)
    Move(B,0,-1,0)
    Move(C,0,-1,0)
    Goto next-column
    🔗
    Follow it with the first grid picture in view. Seven passes the first test and eight passes the second. We write five, move the carry place left and write one, then move the two number places and the answer place left. We have reached the second picture. If either test failed, the next line would send us to the other pairs instead. At next-column, the carried one selects the addition table with an incoming carry. A label is simply a place to resume reading the instructions.
    🔗
    This fragment is one doorway into a larger sheet. Its other doorways handle the other entries, including blanks and the finishing cases. The subtraction tables can be written out in the same manner. The complete sheets live in Appendix A; they are long because a person can know seven plus eight without being told what to do when the seven turns out to be a six. Our worker must be told.
    🔗
    On page three, \(14-8\) has meanwhile succeeded. The two columns have left \(06\text{.}\) Erase the zero and keep the six. That seems simple until we try \(1234-1234\text{,}\) whose columns leave \(0000\text{.}\) Erase every zero and we lose the answer altogether. As we set up this subtraction, let U save the units square of the answer. After success, the working card sits just left of the four zeros. One move right brings it to the first zero. This is not the saved units square, so we erase the zero and move on. The second and third zeros go the same way. At the fourth, the working card reaches the place saved on U. The walk ends with that zero still on the paper:
    \begin{equation*} 000\underline0\ \longrightarrow\ 00\underline0\ \longrightarrow\ 0\underline0\ \longrightarrow\ \underline0. \end{equation*}
    The display omits the blank squares left behind. The underlined zero stays in its original square. With \(098\text{,}\) the first zero goes, but the nine ends the walk before it reaches the saved units square. With \(1002\text{,}\) the first digit ends the walk immediately; neither interior zero is touched. In each case the walk checks whether it has reached the saved last digit or a nonzero digit before erasing anything. The place check has a short written form: IfSame(A,U) Goto finished. It compares the addresses on the two cards. Our four-zero example shows why this test comes before the erasure.
    🔗
    Now our four trials can keep their separate pages. A card identifies the last accepted remainder, and it changes only after success:
    🔗
    page trial count remainder kept
    1 \(30-8=22\) 1 22 on page 1
    2 \(22-8=14\) 2 14 on page 2
    3 \(14-8=6\) 3 6 on page 3
    4 \(6-8\text{:}\) no 3 6 on page 3
    The failed trial has left writing on page four. It can stay there; we have no reason to trust it with the next round. The count is still three, the remainder card still points to six on page three, and OUT still holds \((0,3,0)\text{.}\) Copy three to that waiting square and move OUT one place right:
    🔗
    Bring down zero beside the accepted six. Sixty takes us back to the trial instructions, with the counting square reset to zero. Seven successful subtractions leave four. Write seven, bring down zero to make forty, and five subtractions leave zero. Our division has reached \(15.375\text{:}\)
    🔗
    Another round starts with zero, refuses its first subtraction and prints its unchanged count, zero. So does the next. The directions continue \(15.375000\ldots\text{,}\) long after a person would usually put the pencil down.
    🔗
    The return to earlier instructions is a loop. It returns the worker to a line on the instruction sheet, while the answer keeps growing on the work page. A fixed piece of writing can therefore direct work that has no last step. What changes from one trip to the next is waiting on the desk: the remainder, the count, the place at which the next digit belongs.
    🔗
    We entered the division partway through, but the same route supplies its beginning. Start with remainder zero and bring down the supplied digits of \(123\) one at a time. The first gives one; no eight fits. Ignore that initial answer zero. Bring down two, making twelve; one eight fits. Bring down three beside the remaining four, making forty-three; five eights fit. The supplied digits have ended, so write the point and begin bringing down zeros. When the integer answer has not begun at all, as in \(2/8\text{,}\) put down \(0.\text{.}\) After the point, every zero counts as a digit and must be kept.
    🔗
    The single counting square will never need ten. Before a new digit comes down, the remainder is smaller than the positive divisor—the failed trial saw to that. For divisor eight, the largest remainder is seven, and even bringing down nine makes only seventy-nine: less than ten eights. For any positive divisor, multiplying a smaller remainder by ten and adding at most nine still leaves less than ten copies of that divisor. Nine successful trials are the most a round can have; one further trial must fail. The requirement that the divisor be positive matters. Subtracting zero would keep our worker occupied without getting the pencil any nearer the answer.
    🔗
    Each trial crosses finitely many columns. Each copy crosses finitely many digits. A round contains only finitely many trials. Consequently every particular answer digit arrives after finite work, even when the entire decimal never ends. This is what we will need from a printer, rather more than the ability to keep somebody busy forever. The appendix’s full division sheet makes a few economies: its cards can read the accepted remainder and divisor where they already lie, and it reuses one square for the current borrow. The fresh trial answer still gets its own paper, and a failed trial still leaves the accepted remainder alone.
    🔗
    Alan Turing (1912–1954)
    🔗
    In 1936, Turing compared the squared paper of his human computer to a child’s arithmetic book. His analysis asked which symbols the person observes, which marks can change, and where attention moves. He then simplified the paper to a strip of squares.
     2 
    Turing, “On Computable Numbers,” section 9, pp. 249–251.
    We have kept a more familiar desk, but the little actions have emerged from the same activity. The address numbers themselves can be changed by ordinary addition and subtraction. A fixed collection of place cards can serve us however far the work spreads.
    🔗
    Turing’s table includes the current state, meaning the stage of the directions, alongside the symbol under attention. An entry specifies a new symbol, a move, and a next state:
    🔗
    state symbol write move next state
    a 0 1 right b
    a 1 0 left a
    At stage a, a zero asks for one, a move right, and a jump to stage b. A one asks for zero, a move left, and a return to a. Our worker can follow any finite table like this: the state picks a part of the sheet, the observed symbol picks an entry, and its writes, moves and jump carry out the change. Following the addition tables has already supplied the method.
    🔗
    A finite sheet of instructions is a program. It has finitely many card names and uses a fixed finite collection of marks. Each jump names a line on the sheet; otherwise we continue to the following line. Halt tells the worker to stop. Skill and imagination can go into devising a program, but following it need not reproduce the thoughts of its inventor. That is why somebody else can take the chair.
    🔗
    Just before we copied three after \(15.\text{,}\) the desk contained the accepted six, the rejected scratch work and the place cards. Add the next instruction to this description and we have a configuration: enough to resume the calculation. After the write, three appears at \((0,3,0)\) and the next instruction changes. After the move, the three remains and OUT says \((0,4,0)\text{.}\) A wrong digit or a moved remainder is now a specific disagreement with what the instruction allowed.
    🔗
    We can record configurations in sequence, making a trace. Each configuration needs only a finite list of written squares and their marks, the addresses on the cards, and the next instruction. A trace is therefore a sequence of finite sequences. To check one step, inspect the changes the instruction permits and check that the rest stayed put. This will let us examine calculations as well as perform them.
    🔗
    The worker can leave again. Our directions will find the next digit, and the next, without further advice. Yet what lies on the desk at any moment is still a finite beginning. We have learned how to keep writing a decimal. We have not yet asked how much an unfinished one can tell us.
