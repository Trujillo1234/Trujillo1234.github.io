    Section A.8 The exact form of words and execution records

    Copying instructions brings punctuation into the calculation. A space inside a word, a blank square, and the gap after a word cannot simply be treated as the same nothing. The conventions below give each a definite job. Arithmetic rows still use one ordinary digit per square; general words use a fixed encoding that distinguishes every permitted character, including spaces and punctuation.
    🔗

    Subsection A.8.1 Character squares and their saved ends

    A code table lets us copy letters with the same two marks used for any other coded text. In this table each character has seven squares, each containing zero or one. Such a zero-or-one mark is called a bit. The table is the familiar convention called ASCII; using it here requires lookup, not arithmetic in a new number system.
    🔗
    For example, the entry for M is 1001101. Write those seven marks in seven consecutive squares. The next letter starts in the eighth square. The four letters of MATH become
    🔗
    M A T H
    1001101 1000001 1010100 1001000
    The dividing lines show the groups; they are not extra marks on the row. To read the word back, take seven squares at a time and find the matching entry. The first group gives M again. A space has its own seven marks, 0100000; it is different from unused, blank paper. We use the line-feed entry for a new line.
    🔗
    The complete table below supplies every character permitted in these general words: the printable characters, space, and new line. Other seven-square patterns are rejected.
     1 
    The character assignments are tabulated in Vint Cerf, ASCII format for Network Interchange, RFC 20 (1969), rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc20.html.
    🔗
    mark code mark code mark code
    new line 0001010 ? 0111111 _ 1011111
    space 0100000 @ 1000000 ` 1100000
    ! 0100001 A 1000001 a 1100001
    " 0100010 B 1000010 b 1100010
    # 0100011 C 1000011 c 1100011
    $ 0100100 D 1000100 d 1100100
    % 0100101 E 1000101 e 1100101
    & 0100110 F 1000110 f 1100110
    ’ 0100111 G 1000111 g 1100111
    ( 0101000 H 1001000 h 1101000

    * 0101010 J 1001010 j 1101010
    + 0101011 K 1001011 k 1101011
    , 0101100 L 1001100 l 1101100
    - 0101101 M 1001101 m 1101101
    . 0101110 N 1001110 n 1101110
    / 0101111 O 1001111 o 1101111
    0 0110000 P 1010000 p 1110000
    1 0110001 Q 1010001 q 1110001

    A general word with \(n\) characters occupies \(7n\) squares: seven times as many squares as letters, punctuation marks, and spaces. For MATH, that is 28 squares. If its first square has column zero, its last has column 27, and its saved end card records column 28, just past the writing. A character move advances seven squares. Character copy writes those seven marks before advancing to the next character; comparison checks all seven. Removing a character erases its seven-square block.
    🔗
    Counting still increases once per decoded character. Consequently MATH! has length five. A decimal digit in a general word has a seven-square code, whereas a digit on an arithmetic row has its familiar one-square form. Ten finite branches convert one form to the other. The kind of row is specified when a routine is prepared.
    🔗
    For general program runs, use separate pages for the supplied word, the returned word, the displayed answer, and the remaining work. The supplied and returned words have fixed beginning places and saved end cards. The returned word initially is empty. No end mark is written into the character row. Appending uses the saved end place; clearing sets that place back to the beginning.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A.8.2 Settled output

    For the standardized printers used in the later arguments, number the successive digit places on the answer row \(0,1,2,\ldots\text{.}\) An output card begins at the first of these places. Emit(d) writes digit \(d\) there and moves the card one square right. Its ten possible branches are fixed basic writes and moves. EmitString(s) follows the supplied finite word and performs the corresponding branch for each digit.
    🔗
    A decimal display may place its initial 0. in the two squares immediately to the left. The successive digit places then hold its fractional digits. For a numeral printer, those same successive places hold the numeral itself. The later construction specifies which of these readings is intended. In either case, an emitted digit is never revised. The schoolroom division sheet has its ordinary display, including its integer part and point; its fractional digits are the digits after that point.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A.8.3 Lists of words

    A record containing several words first gives the number of parts followed by a colon. Each part then gives its character length, a colon, and its characters. For example, the words 8 and 123 are recorded as 2:1:83:123. After reading 1:, the worker takes exactly one character; after 3:, exactly three. A colon among those specified characters belongs to the data. Counts have ordinary decimal notation without leading zeros. The worker can count down with the subtraction sheet to locate the end of each part. Missing characters, invalid lengths, or extra characters after the complete record cause rejection. The whole record is stored using the character encoding above. Lists nested inside lists use the same rule.
    🔗
    Read the example once with a finger, separating its jobs:
    🔗
    part read what the worker now knows
    2: there are two words to collect
    1: the first word has one character
    8 the first word is eight
    3: the second word has three characters
    123 the second word is one, two, three
    The character 3 after 8 starts the next length; it does not continue the first word, because that word’s one character has already been taken. If a word itself is A:B, its part is 3:A:B: after reading the length, take three characters, including the colon between the letters. The length tells us when to treat a colon as data rather than as a separator. Even punctuation sometimes has to wait its turn to give an instruction.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A.8.4 Recording one moment and following one line

    A configuration records the current instruction number, the positions on the program’s finitely many named cards, and the finite list of nonblank paper squares. A square entry gives \((p,x,y,a)\text{:}\) page, column, row, and mark. Put entries in increasing page order, then column order, then row order, with no duplicate positions. The instruction sheet fixes the order of the named cards in their list. All coordinates are written as signed decimal numerals.
    🔗
    To follow Write, find the position named by the relevant card, then insert, replace, or erase its square entry. To follow Move, add the three stated fixed shifts to that card’s coordinates. To follow Place, copy one card’s three coordinates to the other. A square test searches the finite list, reading blank when the position has no entry. A same-place test compares the two triples. A jump changes the instruction number. A halt is recognized directly from the indicated line.
    🔗
    For signed addition, two numbers with the same sign add their magnitudes and keep that sign. For different signs, subtract the smaller magnitude from the larger and keep the larger magnitude’s sign. Equal magnitudes give zero without a minus sign. Thus adding 3 to \(-2\) gives \(3-2=1\text{.}\) Comparing signed numbers puts negative numbers below nonnegative numbers; two negative numbers reverse the order of their magnitudes. These finite sign choices and the arithmetic sheets supply the coordinate updates and the ordered insertion of new square entries.
    🔗
    Every one of these searches and updates finishes on a finite record. They provide InitialConfiguration, which prepares the first record; Step, which follows one instruction; NextConfiguration, which takes that step’s new record; and Halted, which tests for a halt. Step can also report a newly settled output digit. A finite run is a finite list of these configurations, each itself made of finite lists.
    🔗
    To follow a supplied program for many steps, keep its record on one’s own work pages, make one update, and repeat. This gives an interpreter. Its own instructions and work occupy different places from the encoded squares of the program it is following. If the supplied program orders an erasure, the interpreter changes the corresponding entry. It has no reason to rub out its own instructions. The separation is as practical as keeping a worked example beside the page on which we are checking it.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A.8.5 Malformed answers and arbitrary programs

    Nothing stops us from writing an instruction sheet that makes a mess. A program can leave half a character, put its end card on the wrong row, or alter a digit it was supposed to have settled. Its raw steps still have definite meanings. We need to recognize what it has actually done without quietly tidying the answer on its behalf. At a halt, inspect the finite record of the returned-word page and its saved end card. A valid end is on the specified row, at a nonnegative multiple of seven from the fixed beginning; the intervening squares contain exactly complete permitted character codes, and no other nonblank square occurs on that page. The finite end coordinate gives a finite search bound, and the finite square list supplies the check for other marks. An invalid arrangement returns a distinguished “malformed result” report. The actual halt is recognized in either case.
    🔗
    Likewise, a settled-output write must put the next digit in its designated place without changing any earlier output square. The interpreter keeps its own record of the next permitted place. If a simulated program violates the rule, the interpreter records the violation and suppresses subsequent output forwarding. It continues to follow every raw instruction of that program. A private simulation testing whether a program halts can ignore output reports entirely. Thus these checks never turn a nonhalting raw program into a halting one, or prevent the recognition of an actual halt.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A.8.6 Constructing exactly the same instruction sheet

    Two sheets can do the same thing without being the same sheet. The self-description argument needs the stronger agreement: every character must match. Here are the complete compact wrappers for Print and Combine. They are macro sheets: Load and Run name the finite copying and simulation procedures described above, not additional elementary operations. Each displayed line has one new-line character after it. Apart from characters inside a literal field, there are no spaces on these wrapper lines. Case, punctuation, line order, and the names of boxes are exactly as printed.
    🔗
    A literal field has the form n:s. The numeral \(n\) is the number of characters in \(s\text{,}\) written without redundant zeros; the empty field is 0:. After the colon, take exactly \(n\) characters. All of them are data, including parentheses, commas, colons, spaces, or new lines. Only after those characters does the closing parenthesis belong to the enclosing command. Thus Load(RESULT,3:A:B) copies the three-character word A:B. The word A) has length two and gives the line Load(RESULT,2:A)): the first closing parenthesis is copied data, the second closes the command. Nested literal fields therefore do not require a new kind of quotation.
    🔗
    To prepare Print(s), replace n:s in this fixed wrapper by the length and complete text of \(s\text{:}\)
    🔗
    Load(RESULT,n:s)
Halt
    For the one-character word A, the complete compact sheet is
    🔗
    Load(RESULT,1:A)
Halt
    Load replaces the destination word, resetting its beginning and end cards, then copying exactly the literal characters. It advances the end by seven physical squares per character and returns to the following wrapper line. Consequently this sheet returns A on every input. It emits no settled digits. Its elementary expansion clears the result row, writes the seven bits 1000001, saves the end seven squares after the beginning, and halts.
    🔗
    To prepare Combine(p,q), replace m:p by the length and complete text of \(p\text{,}\) and n:q by the length and complete text of \(q\text{,}\) in precisely this wrapper:
    🔗
    Load(P,m:p)
Load(Q,n:q)
Run(P,INPUT,Y,LEFT)
Run(Q,Y,RESULT,RIGHT)
Halt
    For the elementary texts 0: Halt and 0: Goto 0, neither with a final new line, this produces
    🔗
    Load(P,7:0: Halt)
Load(Q,9:0: Goto 0)
Run(P,INPUT,Y,LEFT)
Run(Q,Y,RESULT,RIGHT)
Halt
    The preparation copies five wrapper lines and two finite literal texts. It runs neither supplied program. In execution the first simulation returns an empty word; the second repeats its jump forever. Preparing these five lines a second time gives the same literal fields 7:0: Halt and 9:0: Goto 0, followed by the same three lines and the same new lines. Every character agrees.
    🔗

    Arguments, work areas, and returns..

    Every invocation has its own frame: a separate record containing the words INPUT, RESULT, P, Q, and Y, together with the simulated records LEFT and RIGHT. INPUT is a copy of the caller’s supplied word. All other word boxes start empty; the two simulation records start unused. These are named entries in a finite record, stored with the list conventions above. A nested invocation gets a new record, rather than reusing its caller’s boxes. Its frame is kept with the caller’s saved next-line number until it returns. This is the paper counterpart of keeping one unfinished worksheet while opening another.
    🔗
    Run(C,X,Z,W) reads the program text in box C and a copy of the input word in box X. It initializes the private record W with that program’s starting cards, supplied word, empty returned-word row, and otherwise blank simulated paper. It repeatedly applies the one-step rule. A valid halt copies the entire returned word, including its end position, to Z, then resumes at the wrapper line immediately following this Run. It does not jump to the caller’s own halt. A run that continues forever never returns. P and Q remain the same supplied texts; simulated erasures alter only the corresponding record in LEFT or RIGHT.
    🔗
    Settled digits from a valid simulated output are forwarded in order to the invocation’s output channel. The second run continues after all digits forwarded by the first; its own recorded output positions still start at zero. Temporary word boxes are never forwarded as digits. A malformed returned word stops the wrapper with the same distinguished malformed-result report defined above; it does not start the next simulation with an invented input. A simulated output violation is handled by the earlier suppression convention while raw simulation continues. Reaching the wrapper’s own Halt returns its RESULT to its caller, or ends the whole calculation when there is no caller.
    🔗
    🔗

    Compact sheets and elementary descriptions..

    The placeholders m:p, n:q, and n:s are preparation slots, not characters left in the finished sheet. Counting and copying supply every character in each finished compact wrapper. Inside a literal, the supplied text is preserved verbatim; it is never renumbered, reformatted, or expanded by the copying step. When the supplied text includes a new line, that character contributes one to its length even though its seven-square code occupies seven physical squares.
    🔗
    As elsewhere in the book, the complete program description means the elementary instruction sheet after expansion. Fix the elementary copying and interpreter routines once, before any texts are supplied. Expand wrapper lines in their printed order, and number new instruction labels in increasing order as they are emitted; use the same fixed naming of physical marks and cards on every occasion. A general Call F(arguments,result) makes a private frame for the fixed routine F, copies the listed arguments into its input fields in that order, saves the following caller line, and resumes there after copying the returned result. It does not substitute argument characters into instructions to be obeyed. More than one incoming word is packed as a list of words using the specified length fields.
    🔗
    The complete elementary expansion of the interpreter is not printed. The wrapper and all its literal slots are printed: the reader can assemble and compare those compact sheets, and follow their effects with the simulation rules. Equality of compact sheets implies equality of their elementary expansions under the one fixed expansion procedure. In particular, if the supplied text is the completed elementary white sheet of Chapter 4, both preparations copy that same text into the same slots. Both give the identical expanded blue sheet. This is the textual identity needed in the self-description argument; it does not depend on different programs merely having the same behavior.
